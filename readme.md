# A Structural Analysis of Class Reproduction in the Dutch Student Finance and Housing System
## The Mechanism Is Not Accidental—It Is Enforced

**Abstract**

This paper examines the proposition that the Dutch state operates a system of enforcement that is not accidental but designed — not as a single blueprint, but as dynamic legislation and policy brought forth from doctrine — to reward certain classes while excluding others. The analysis emerges from a first-person account of navigating the Dutch education and housing system, and it tests that account against documented evidence of state practices. The paper argues that the mechanism of enforcement operates through three interconnected instruments: (1) discriminatory algorithmic profiling by state agencies, (2) punitive welfare fraud legislation that criminalises administrative error, and (3) a moral hierarchy rooted in Calvinist notions of chosenness that legitimises the exclusion of outsiders while protecting the established class. To illustrate the structural asymmetry, the paper contrasts three student living scenarios: (a) living with lower-class parents, (b) living independently on DUO with €900 rent, and (c) living in a home purchased by wealthy parents who rent rooms to other students, generating 3–4 times the student room income as additional monthly income for their child.

**The central argument of this paper concerns the "average rent." The average rent is not a market price. It is a network price. It describes the rent paid by students who have gained access to the fraternity (*studentenvereniging*) allocation system—a system gated by social compliance rather than money. The documented forms of that compliance include *ontgroening* (hazing: forced alcohol consumption, physical exhaustion, humiliation, degradation); sexual ranking and coercion, documented in the *bangalijst* scandals at Amsterdam, Utrecht, and Groningen; and participation in the informal economies of fraternity houses, including documented drug use and distribution. The ordinary member may pay only in hazing and social conformity, but the continuum of compliance runs to these documented extremes. A student who declines to pay this non-monetary entry fee is not offered the average rent. They are offered the open market rent, which in Amsterdam exceeds €950 and which constitutes the top quintile of the distribution. The statistics that report an "average" therefore describe a subsidised price available only to those inside the network, while concealing the penalty paid by those outside it.**

The evidence confirms that the system produces materially different outcomes for those with family capital and those without. **The central finding is that there is no upwards mobility for the student without parental support (a home) and without network access. It is the system functioning as designed to exclude those without family capital or social compliance.** Furthermore, the statistics that purport to measure the system's functioning are structurally skewed by the inclusion of international students, wealthy independents, and—crucially—fraternity members, concealing the devastation experienced by self-reliant Dutch students.

## 1. Introduction

The preceding conversation established a structural argument: the Netherlands operates a system where the rich class can afford honesty, the working class is pressured into survival, and the state enforces a moral hierarchy that positions the established Dutch elite as legally and morally superior. This paper tests that argument against documented evidence. It is self-referential in that it takes the conversation itself as its point of departure, treating the first-person account as a hypothesis to be examined against institutional records, parliamentary inquiries, and scholarly analysis.

The question is not whether individual Dutch people are honest or fraudulent. The question is whether the system—the laws, algorithms, enforcement practices, housing allocation mechanisms, and cultural narratives—produces predictable outcomes that favour certain groups and disadvantage others. The evidence suggests that it does. The consequence is the effective exclusion of students who lack parental support in the form of housing, and who lack access to the social networks that gate the affordable segment of the housing market.

The distinction between "failing to help" and "designed to exclude" is analytically important. A system that fails to help is negligent. A system whose every instrument—grant structure, housing market regulation, fraud enforcement, network-based allocation, labour market access, and moral narrative—converges on the same outcome is functioning as designed. This paper argues that the Dutch student system is the latter.

The design is not a single blueprint. It is dynamic legislation and policy brought forth from a doctrine that specifies who belongs and who does not. The doctrine is the Calvinist inheritance that distinguishes the elect from the reprobate, the tested from the failed, the insider from the outsider. The legislation and policy are its expressions. The exclusion is their function.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: The State as a Disciplining Apparatus

The theoretical foundation for this analysis draws on the observation that the welfare state and the criminal justice system are not separate entities but function as disciplining institutions that serve the same state and are influenced by the same politics. When welfare provisions shrink, punitive measures expand; as the state retracts support, it simultaneously tightens its criminal justice grip.

Abramovitz (2023) argues that this convergence has eroded citizens' trust in the state: as workfare has shifted to a punitive model, and its regulatory gaps have been filled with a significant expansion of the penal state, manifested in prison overcrowding, increasing criminalization, and the growth of penitentiary institutions. Although the welfare state and the criminal justice system are described as separate entities, they serve the same state, and are influenced by the same politics dominant in that state. This convergence is evident not only in organization practices and policymaking cultures, but also in overarching political goals established in the government. When governments pursue their inhabitants to work more, welfare measures are designed to push inhabitants towards labour market participation. Therefore, they both also serve the disciplining and controlling features of that state.

This framework is essential for understanding the Dutch case. The Netherlands has constructed an extensive apparatus for detecting and punishing fraud, but this apparatus is not neutral. It is targeted. The question is: at whom? And the answer is that it targets those who lack the family capital to absorb risk—precisely those for whom upwards mobility is most necessary and most difficult.

The convergence is the point. Grant amounts that do not cover rent, housing markets that require guarantors, fraud algorithms that flag foreignness, welfare laws that criminalise error, network-gated allocation of affordable rooms, and labour markets mediated by *kruiwagen* networks—these are not independent policy failures. They are a single architecture. Each instrument produces the same outcome: the student without family capital and without network access is filtered out.

## 3. The True Cost of Study: Beyond Rent

Before examining the three living scenarios, it is necessary to establish what a student actually costs per month in the Netherlands. The €70 remainder after rent, which grounded the original analysis, measured only one cost category. The full cost of being a student is substantially larger.

### 3.1 The Nibud Minimum Budget (2024)

The Nibud Studentenonderzoek 2024, the standard reference used by Dutch universities, provides the following monthly cost breakdown for an *uitwonende* (living independently) HBO/WO student:

| Cost Category | Monthly Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent | €561 |
| Groceries | €244 |
| Books and study materials | €41 |
| Transport (beyond OV card) | €67 |
| Leisure, entertainment, sports | €239 |
| Clothing and shoes | €60 |
| Health insurance | €156 |
| Phone | €16 |
| Tuition (collegegeld) | €218 |
| **Total (including tuition)** | **€1,602** |

Source: Nibud 2024, as reported by SUSA and Maastricht University.

### 3.2 The Realistic Budget

The Nibud figures are minimum example budgets. In practice, irregular costs—social occasions, larger purchases, spontaneous expenses—add €200 to €300 per month, bringing the realistic total to **€1,800–€1,900 per month**.

### 3.3 The Network Rent and Its Compliance Price

The Nibud average rent of €561 masks extreme variation. Kamernet's rental reports, based on thousands of rooms across Dutch student cities, found that in the second quarter of 2025, the national average student room rent was **€601 per month**. However, this average conceals the reality in major student cities:

| City | Average Room Rent (2025) |
|---|---|
| Amsterdam | €945–€979 |
| Utrecht | €762–€803 |
| Rotterdam | €744–€756 |
| Haarlem | €770 |

Source: Kamernet data, as reported by BNNVARA, Omroep Gelderland, and DutchReview.

**The critical point is that these "averages" are not market prices in any ordinary sense.** They are network prices. A substantial portion of the affordable student housing supply—rooms in fraternity-owned buildings, rooms allocated through fraternity and association networks, rooms passed down through closed circles—is available only to students who have gained access to those networks. The average is therefore subsidised by a segment of the market that non-members cannot enter.

A rent of **€900 per month** is not exceptional. It is within the normal range for Amsterdam and above average but plausible in Utrecht and Rotterdam. But the student who pays €900 is not paying the average. They are paying the **residual market price**—the price that remains after the network has taken its share. In Amsterdam, that residual price exceeds €950. In the general distribution, it constitutes the top quintile.

**The price of access to the network rent is not money. It is compliance.** This is the mechanism that the statistics conceal.

#### 3.3.1 The Mechanics of Network Access

The *hospiteren* system—where current residents choose their new housemate from a pool of candidates—is not a neutral selection process. It is a mechanism of social reproduction. DUWO, one of the largest student housing providers in the Netherlands with over 33,000 rooms, has explicitly acknowledged that "some students have far fewer chances of getting a room than others, for example because they are not members of a student association". The housing provider states that "students without a network, with less access to information or without a connection to certain associations, have significantly less chance of getting a room".

This is not a marginal effect. In Leiden, nearly **40% of *hospiteer* rooms** are exclusively occupied by members of the four largest fraternities—Minerva, Augustinus, Quintus, and Njord—which together represent only 16% of Leiden students. The SSH director, Rob Donninger, has admitted that the *hospiteren* system "structurally excludes certain groups of students" and that there is "an increasing inequality" between groups that cannot be justified by a "competitive housing market" alone.

The practical consequence is a dual housing market. Students who join fraternities—engaging in *ontgroening* and social conformity—gain access to a network that provides **below-market or average-priced rooms**, often in fraternity-owned buildings. As one fraternity member explained, "With the choir you often had housing guarantee... Usually you have a good chance that you'll get it". The network actively redirects rejected candidates to other houses: "Then they say: 'we don't see it happening', but they do think along with you... Then you get forwarded to other houses".

Students who are not members, who do not wish to participate in this system, or who are excluded from it—including international students, MBO students, and those without social connections—are not candidates for these rooms. They are forced into the open market, where private landlords and commercial platforms operate. **This is where the €900+ rents reside.**

#### 3.3.2 The Compliance Continuum

The entry fee to the network is not a single act. It is a continuum of social compliance, and the documented forms of that compliance run from the routine to the extreme.

**At the routine end** is *ontgroening*: the hazing period that new members of Dutch student associations must endure. This is not a myth or an exaggeration. The excesses of Dutch hazing have been the subject of national parliamentary debate, criminal investigation, and repeated reform attempts. Documented practices include forced alcohol consumption to the point of hospitalization, physical exhaustion, sleep deprivation, humiliation, degradation, and the performance of degrading acts. The Vindicat association in Groningen was the subject of a 2016–2017 investigation that documented hazing practices including forced drinking and sexualized degradation. Similar scandals have recurred at associations across the country.

The function of *ontgroening* is not initiation in any benign sense. It is a **filter**. It selects for those willing to endure humiliation in exchange for belonging—which is to say, it selects for those who will pay the compliance price for access to the network's resources, including housing.

**At the documented extreme end** are two phenomena that have reached national attention:

1. **The *bangalijst* scandals.** In 2021, 2022, and 2023, sexual ranking lists ("bang lists") were discovered at fraternities in Amsterdam, Utrecht, and Groningen. These lists ranked female members and guests by sexual attractiveness and, in some cases, by sexual availability, and were circulated among male members. The scandals at the Amsterdam student association ASC/AVSV, the Utrecht associations USSV and UVSV, and the Groningen association Vindicat prompted investigations and, in some cases, criminal proceedings. The documented allegations included sexual coercion, the use of social pressure to obtain sexual compliance, and the operation of a culture in which women's access to the social and material resources of the association was conditional on their compliance with the sexual expectations of senior members.

2. **The informal economies of fraternity houses.** Fraternity houses in the Netherlands have been the subject of documented drug-related incidents. These include the distribution of cocaine and other substances within and through fraternity networks, the use of fraternity houses as distribution points, and the participation of members in the informal economy that surrounds student drug use. In 2018, a fraternity at Vindicat was implicated in cocaine distribution. Similar incidents have been documented at associations in other cities.

**The paper does not claim that every fraternity member deals drugs, nor that every female student who joins an association is subjected to sexual coercion.** That would be an unsupported generalisation. **The paper claims something narrower and more structural: the allocation mechanism for the affordable segment of the student housing market is a closed network, and access to that network is purchased not with money but with social compliance. The documented forms of that compliance include hazing, sexual ranking and coercion, and participation in informal economies including drug distribution. The ordinary member may pay only in hazing and social conformity, but the continuum runs to these documented extremes, and the network does not exclude those who engage in them—it rewards them with access.**

The student who declines to pay the compliance price at all—who does not wish to be hazed, does not wish to participate in or tolerate the ranking and coercion of women, does not wish to participate in the informal economies of the house—is not offered the network rent. They are offered the open market rent. In Amsterdam, this exceeds €950. In the residual market generally, it is the top quintile of the distribution.

**This is the mechanism that the "average rent" statistic conceals.** The average is pulled down by the substantial volume of rooms allocated through compliance-gated networks. The average is not available to those who decline the compliance price. The student who declines is not an average case; they are a **forced high-price case**. The rent they face is not the average but the price of the residual market that remains after the network has taken its share.

And the statistics report the average as if it were a market price available to all. The student who pays €900 is told, implicitly, that they are paying an above-average rent because they failed to secure a better deal. **The truth is that they were never offered the better deal. The better deal is not a deal. It is a membership benefit, and the membership fee is paid in compliance.**

This is not a matter of choice. A student who does not engage in the fraternity system—whether for reasons of principle, personality, or exclusion—is effectively priced out of the average and forced into the highest 20% of the market. The system does not merely fail to help them; it **structurally penalises** them for their non-participation.

For a student paying €900 rent—a rent that is **above average but representative of the non-network market**—the rent alone exceeds **110% of the maximum DUO grant** (basic + supplementary, approximately €805–€815) and consumes **90% of the €1,000 total DUO income** assumed in the self-referential case.

### 3.4 The Structural Deficit

For the self-referential case, the parameters are:

- DUO income: **€1,000 per month**
- Rent: **€900 per month**

Measured against the full Nibud budget, the deficit is:

| Cost Category | Monthly Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent | €900 |
| Groceries | €244 |
| Books | €41 |
| Transport | €67 |
| Leisure | €239 |
| Clothing | €60 |
| Health insurance | €156 |
| Phone | €16 |
| **Total (excluding tuition)** | **€1,723** |
| **DUO income** | **€1,000** |
| **Monthly deficit (excl. tuition)** | **−€723** |
| **Monthly deficit (incl. tuition ~€218)** | **−€941** |

The €100 remainder after rent is not a student budget. It is **22.4% of the minimum non-rent costs** (approximately €447 for food, transport, books, phone) and **5.6% of the total minimum budget**. The structural deficit is not €100 per month. It is **€723 to €941 per month**—a gap of roughly **€8,676 to €11,292 per year** that the system does not cover and that the student must bridge through loans, work, or family capital.

A student without family capital has no third option. They have loans (which accumulate) or work (which competes with study time). The system provides no path that does not require one of these. This is the first instrument of exclusion.

## 4. The Chain of Beneficiaries: The Fraternity System as a Mechanism of Class Reproduction

The fraternity system is not a social club that happens to own property. It is a property-holding corporation that happens to have social functions. Its assets are real estate. Its members are the children of property owners. Its function is to ensure that property remains within the network.

### 4.1 The Direct Beneficiaries: The Landowning Class and Its Heirs

The mechanism is straightforward. A parent with capital buys a property in a student city for €400,000–€600,000. The child occupies one bedroom. The remaining four rooms are rented to other students at €650–€800 per month. Gross rental income is €2,600–€3,200 per month. The mortgage is covered. The property appreciates. By graduation, the family owns an appreciating asset financed by the rents of other students. The child pays zero rent and graduates with equity. The students who paid the mortgage graduate with debt.

This is not a marginal phenomenon. It is the primary mechanism through which student housing is provided in the Netherlands. The state does not build enough student housing. Social housing waitlists run years. The private market fills the gap. And the private market is capital-gated: only those with capital can enter as suppliers. The parents of fraternity members are disproportionately those with capital.

The fraternities defend this system as "culture." The LKvV, the national fraternity umbrella, sent an urgent letter to DUWO fighting to retain the *hospiteren* system. They frame their opposition as a defense of "Dutch student culture" and "social cohesion." But what they are defending is property. The culture is the alibi. The property is the interest.

The rich kid in the fraternity is not a victim of the hazing. He is a beneficiary of it. The hazing is the price of admission to the network. The network is the mechanism through which property is protected. The property is the wealth. The wealth is the power. The hazing is the ritual through which the class reproduces itself.

### 4.2 The Land Value They Prop Up

The presence of fraternity-owned and parent-owned properties in student cities props up land values. When wealthy parents buy properties for their children, they increase demand. When demand increases, prices rise. When prices rise, the average rent rises. When the average rent rises, students without family capital are priced out.

The fraternities are not passive beneficiaries of this process. They are active participants. They buy properties. They renovate them. They rent them to members. They maintain the network that keeps the properties within the class. They set the standard for what student housing looks like — and the standard is high, because the standard is set by people with capital.

The statistics then report an "average rent" that is pulled down by the fraternity-controlled segment of the market. The average is not the price that non-members pay. It is the price that members pay — and members pay less because they are subsidized by the network. The non-member pays the residual price. The residual price is higher. The statistics conceal this. The student who pays €900 is told they are paying an above-average rent because they failed to secure a better deal. The truth is they were never offered the better deal. The better deal is not a deal. It is a membership benefit. And the membership fee is paid in compliance.

### 4.3 The Indirect Beneficiaries: The Average Students Who Get Average Rooms

The average room is not available to everyone. It is available to students who have some access to the network — not necessarily the top fraternities, but some network. They may be members of a smaller association. They may have friends who are members. They may have parents who know someone. They may have been in the right place at the right time.

These students are not the rich kids. They do not own property. They do not have a *kruiwagen* that will carry them into the labour market. But they have enough access to get a room at the average price. They are the system's middle layer. They are not the beneficiaries of the system in the way the rich kids are. But they are beneficiaries relative to those who are excluded.

Their complicity takes three forms.

**First, they participate in the compliance continuum.** The compliance continuum runs from hazing to sexual ranking to informal economies. The average student may not participate in the extremes. But they participate in the routine. They attend the parties. They laugh at the jokes. They keep silent about the things that happen in the house. They do not challenge the system because the system is giving them a room.

**Second, they maintain the silence.** The system depends on silence. The aspirant who reports is a traitor. The member who speaks out is a problem. The average student knows this. They may not like the system. They may not agree with the hazing. But they do not speak. They do not report. They do not organize. They accept the room and the silence that comes with it.

**Third, they blame the excluded.** The average student who gets a room may look at the student who does not and think: "They didn't try hard enough." "They didn't network." "They didn't apply in time." The average student does not see that the system is designed to exclude. They see individual failure. They see the excluded student's failure to secure a room as the excluded student's problem. This is the moral hierarchy's final instrument: it makes the included complicit in the exclusion by convincing them that the exclusion is deserved.

The average student can deny their complicity. They can say they were not responsible for the system. They can say they just found a room. They can say they did not design the *hospiteren* system. They can say they did not buy the property. They can say they are not the rich kid. All of this is true. But it is also irrelevant. The system does not need the average student to design it. It needs the average student to accept it. It needs the average student to take the room, keep silent, and blame the excluded. The average student's deniability is the system's oxygen.

### 4.4 The Chain of Deniability

The chain of deniability runs through every actor in the system.

The **landlord** says: "I just rent rooms. The market sets the price."

The **fraternity** says: "We just have traditions. We are a social club."

The **rich kid** says: "My parents just helped me. I worked hard too."

The **average student** says: "I just found a room. I didn't design the system."

The **board** says: "We just set the framework. The members run the activities."

The ***ouderejaars*** says: "I just followed tradition. I was hazed too."

The **university** says: "We just provide education. Student life is not our responsibility."

The **municipality** says: "We just enforce the law. We cannot intervene in private housing."

The **state** says: "We just provide grants and loans. The market provides housing."

Everyone is involved. No one is guilty. This is the tragic comedy. The excluded student is not excluded by any single actor. They are excluded by the system that every actor maintains while denying responsibility for it.

### 4.5 The Doctrine in Operation

The main paper argues that the Dutch system is designed to exclude by functioning as dynamic legislation and policy brought forth from doctrine. The doctrine is the Calvinist inheritance that distinguishes the elect from the reprobate, the tested from the failed, the insider from the outsider.

The fraternity system is the doctrine in operation. It is the mechanism through which the doctrine is enacted, enforced, and reproduced.

The **elect** are the fraternity members and their families. They have purchased their authority through the compliance continuum. They have inherited the network. They own the property. They set the standard. They control access. They are the insiders. The doctrine says: authority must be purchased. The elect have purchased it.

The **reprobate** are the excluded students. They have not purchased authority. They do not have the network. They do not own property. They pay the non-network price. They are the outsiders. The doctrine says: those who have not purchased are illegitimate. The reprobate have not purchased. They are illegitimate.

The **average student** who gets an average room is the borderline case. They have some access. They have paid some price. They are not fully elect, but they are not fully reprobate. The system needs them to be complicit. The system needs them to accept the room, keep silent, and blame the excluded. The system needs them to believe that they earned their place, even though their place is relative to those who are excluded.

The average student's complicity is the system's most important resource. The rich kids can reproduce the system because they have the capital. The fraternities can gate the system because they have the network. But the system can only function if the average student — the student who is neither rich nor excluded — accepts the terms. The average student's acceptance is the system's legitimacy.

### 4.6 The Chain of Beneficiaries

The chain of complicity is not a metaphor. It is a mechanism. Each actor in the chain benefits from the system in some way. Each actor denies responsibility. Each actor points to another. The chain holds because every link holds.

The **landlord** profits from the rent.

The **fraternity** profits from the network.

The **rich kid** profits from the property.

The **average student** profits from the room.

The **board** profits from the status.

The ***ouderejaars*** profits from the credential.

The **university** profits from the enrollment.

The **municipality** profits from the tax base.

The **state** profits from the loan interest.

The system is not a failure. It is a success. It is producing exactly what it is designed to produce: the reproduction of a class that purchases its authority and excludes those who cannot. The comedy is the system's self-description. The paper's job is to show how the comedy works — and who it serves.

## 5. Three Students, One System: A Controlled Comparison

To isolate the variable that determines a student's outcome in the Netherlands, hold everything constant except family wealth. The same city, the same university, the same programme, the same intelligence, the same work ethic, the same age, the same nationality. The only difference is what the parents own.

**The three students:**

| | Student A | Student B | Student C |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lives with** | Parents (low income) | Alone, rented room | Parents' purchased house |
| **Pays rent** | €0 | €900/month | €0 |
| **Family capital** | None | None | €400,000–€600,000 |
| **Receives rental income** | No | No | Yes, from other students |
| **Network access** | No | No | Yes (inherited) |

This is the entire experiment. Everything that follows is arithmetic.

### 5.1 The Baseline: What DUO Provides

Before the scenarios, establish what the state gives. DUO grant amounts for 2025:

| Component | Living at home (*thuiswonend*) | Living away (*uitwonend*) |
|---|---|---|
| Basic grant | €125.99/month | €314.00/month |
| Supplementary grant (max) | €491/month | €491/month |
| **Maximum grant total** | **€616.99/month** | **€805.00/month** |
| Maximum loan (optional) | €315.17/month | €315.17/month |
| **Maximum possible from DUO** | **€932.16/month** | **€1,120.17/month** |

Source: DUO 2025.

The supplementary grant depends on **parental income from two years prior**. It is not available to students whose parents earn above roughly €70,000. For students whose parents earn below roughly €43,500, the full supplementary grant applies. Crucially, **the supplementary grant is the same amount whether the student lives at home or away**. The only difference DUO recognises is the basic grant: €314.00 versus €125.99, a gap of **€188.01 per month**.

That €188.01 is the state's entire compensation for the cost of moving out.

### 5.2 Student A — Lives With Parents, Low Income

**Income:**
- Basic grant (thuiswonend): €125.99
- Supplementary grant (max, low parental income): €491.00
- **Total: €616.99**

**Costs:**
- Rent: **€0**

**What remains: €616.99**

**What that buys:** Food (€244), transport (€67), books (€41), phone (€16) = **€368 in bare essentials**. Student A has €249 left for clothing, leisure, and everything else, and no rent to pay. This is a tight but **solvent** existence. They will not go hungry. They will not need to borrow to eat.

**What Student A cannot do:** Save meaningfully. Invest. Buy property. Build an asset base. Student A is stable but stationary. They are the **ceiling of the system for students without capital** — and that ceiling is subsistence.

**The hidden constraint:** Student A is dependent on parents who are themselves poor. If the parents need financial help, Student A is expected to provide it. If the household is unstable, Student A has no escape. Student A's stability is contingent on a family situation they did not choose.

### 5.3 Student B — Lives Independently, €900 Rent, No Family Support, No Network Access

**Income:**
- Basic grant (uitwonend): €314.00
- Supplementary grant (max, low parental income): €491.00
- **Total: €805.00**
- Optional loan (max): €315.17
- **Total with maximum loan: €1,120.17**

For this scenario, assume the student receives **€1,000 per month** total (a realistic mid-point between grant-only and grant-plus-maximum-loan).

**Costs:**

| Item | Monthly |
|---|---|
| **Rent** | **€900** |
| Food | €244 |
| Books | €41 |
| Transport | €67 |
| Phone | €16 |
| **Bare minimum subtotal** | **€1,268** |
| Leisure, clothing, health insurance | €455 |
| **Full Nibud minimum subtotal** | **€1,723** |
| Tuition | €218 |
| **Full total** | **€1,941** |

**What remains after rent: €100**

**What that €100 covers:** 22% of the bare minimum non-rent costs (€368). It covers food for about twelve days.

**The deficits:**

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Deficit vs. bare minimum (rent + food + books + transport + phone) | **−€268/month** |
| Deficit vs. full Nibud minimum (excl. tuition) | **−€723/month** |
| Deficit vs. full Nibud minimum (incl. tuition) | **−€941/month** |
| **Annual deficit (excl. tuition)** | **−€8,676** |
| **Annual deficit (incl. tuition)** | **−€11,292** |

**What this means in practice:**

Student B is not "living on a budget." Student B is **funding a deficit**. Every month requires money that does not exist in the DUO allocation. The available sources are:

1. **More debt** — taking the full €315.17 loan still leaves a monthly gap. Debt accumulates against future income, and it must be repaid from a starting position of zero assets.
2. **Work** — hours that compete directly with study time. The student who works 15 hours a week to survive is not the student who can take the unpaid internship, attend the evening lecture, or prepare properly for exams.
3. **Going without** — skipping meals, forgoing books, dropping health insurance, not traveling to campus.

**What Student B cannot do:** Save. Invest. Buy property. Accumulate anything. Student B is on a treadmill, not a ladder. The moment anything goes wrong—illness, a failed course, a rent increase, a family emergency—there is no cushion. Student B falls.

**And Student B's €900 rent is not lost to the void. It is paid to someone.**

**Critically, Student B's rent is not the average rent.** It is the **non-network price**—the price a student pays when they are excluded from the fraternity-controlled segment of the market. The €900 figure is a conservative estimate; in Amsterdam, the non-network price regularly exceeds €950, and the highest 20% of rents are higher still. Student B is not an average case. They are a **forced high-price case**.

### 5.4 Student C — Parents Purchase a House, Student Rents the Rooms

**The setup:** Student C's parents buy a five-bedroom house in a student city for **€400,000–€600,000**. Student C occupies one bedroom. The other four are rented to fellow students at market rate.

**Rental income:**

| Rooms rented | Rate | Monthly gross |
|---|---|---|
| 4 rooms | €650 | €2,600 |
| 4 rooms | €700 | €2,800 |
| 4 rooms | €800 | €3,200 |

**Gross rental income: €2,600–€3,200/month.** This is roughly **2.9 to 3.6 times** the €900 that Student B pays for a single room.

**Student C's rent: €0.** The student owns (via parents) the asset they live in.

**Student C's costs:**
- Rent: **€0**
- Food, books, transport, phone, leisure, clothing, insurance: same as Student B, approximately €823

**Student C's income:**
- DUO basic grant (if claimed, uitwonend): €314.00
- Supplementary grant: depends on parental income. **Parents wealthy enough to buy a €500,000 house will not qualify. Student C receives €0 supplementary grant.** DUO total: €314.00
- Rental income (net, after mortgage/utilities/maintenance): €0–€2,800, depending on financing

**Two financing cases:**

*Case C1: Parents bought outright (no mortgage).* Rental income of €2,800 is nearly all net after utilities and maintenance (say €400) = **€2,400/month net to the student or family**.

*Case C2: Parents took a mortgage.* A €450,000 mortgage at 4% over 30 years costs roughly €2,150/month. Rental income of €2,800 covers the mortgage (€2,150), utilities and maintenance (€400), leaving **€250/month**, while the family builds equity in an appreciating asset. The student still pays €0 rent.

**Either way, Student C's position is:**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent paid | **€0** |
| DUO income | €314 |
| Rental income (net) | €0–€2,400 |
| Costs (living, excl. rent) | −€823 |
| **Monthly position** | **+€250 to +€1,891, or breakeven with asset accumulation** |

**What Student C can do that A and B cannot:**

- Refuse a fraudulent job offer and remain solvent.
- Take an unpaid internship that leads to a career.
- Study full-time without competing work hours.
- Retake a failed course without financial catastrophe.
- Graduate owning, or with family owning, an appreciating asset.
- Enter the labour market with a network already established through the tenants, the property, and the parents' circle.

**The compounding:** The house appreciates. The rent covers the mortgage. The equity belongs to the family. By graduation, Student C's family may have gained tens of thousands in equity while Student B has accumulated tens of thousands in debt. The gap is not static. It widens every month.

### 5.6 The Transfer Mechanism

The three scenarios are not separate. They are connected by a flow of money.

**Student B pays €900 per month in rent.** That money does not disappear. It goes to a landlord. In the Dutch student housing market, a significant and growing share of landlords are **private individuals**—owner-occupiers letting spare rooms, or investors who have bought specifically to let to students. Increasingly, these are the parents of students like Student C, who purchase property for their child and let the remaining rooms.

**Student B's rent is Student C's income.**

The system does not merely permit this transfer. It **structures the housing market so that it is the primary mechanism of student housing provision.** When the state does not build sufficient student housing, and when social housing waitlists run years, the private market fills the gap—and the private market is capital-gated. Only those with capital can enter as suppliers. Everyone else enters as payers.

The result:

- Student C's family acquires an appreciating asset and a monthly income stream.
- Student B pays for it, from a deficit, financed by debt.
- Student B's debt becomes the interest income of a bank, and their rent becomes the equity of Student C's family.
- Student B graduates with a negative net worth. Student C graduates with a positive one.

**The gap is not an accident of individual circumstance. It is the designed output of a system whose legislation and policy are brought forth from doctrine.**

### 5.7 Conclusion of the Comparison

The three students are identical in every respect except family capital and network access. The system produces three outcomes:

- **Student A:** Solvent, stationary. The system's ceiling for those without capital who stay at home.
- **Student B:** Insolvent, in deficit, in survival mode, with no path to asset accumulation. The system's output for those without capital who live independently—which is the precondition for full participation in university life—and who lack network access.
- **Student C:** Solvent, accumulating, with high mobility and a built-in safety net. The system's output for those with capital and inherited network access.

The variable that determines which outcome a student receives is **not effort, intelligence, or merit**. It is whether their parents own property, and whether they inherit access to the network that gates the affordable segment of the housing market.

**There is no upwards mobility for the student without parental support and without network access. The student is not failed by the system. The student is excluded by it — not by a single designer, but by legislation and policy brought forth from a doctrine that specifies who belongs. It is the system functioning as designed to exclude those without family capital or social compliance.**

## 6. The Statistical Skew: How the Independent Student Category Conceals the Crisis

The Dutch student housing debate relies on a headline figure: approximately 44% of HBO and WO students live independently (*op kamers*), while 56% live with their parents. This figure is presented as evidence that the system functions. But the figure is misleading. The stability of the independent category is not the result of Dutch students finding rooms. It is the result of international students filling the gap left by Dutch students who can no longer afford to leave home—and of the fraternity system concealing the true market price faced by non-members.

### 6.1 The Collapse of Dutch Independent Living

Kences, the knowledge centre for student housing, has documented the trend with precision. Eight years ago, 52% of Dutch HBO and WO students lived on rooms. That percentage has steadily declined to **44% this year**. The number of students who say they want to live independently has also fallen, from 59% to 49% over the same period.

Kences sees that students are giving up hope of finding a room. Kences director Jolan de Bie states: "If fewer and fewer students live independently, this limits access to education. If you can't leave home, this generally means longer travel times or having to choose a different study programme because the travel distance is too great." She continues: "Students who cannot find housing and therefore remain living with their parents also miss an important part of their social-emotional development that is crucial for young people in this life phase. But also the experience of being (partly) outside student life can give a feeling of isolation and a lower self-image".

De Bie points out that it becomes much more difficult for students to build an extensive network if they live at home. This also limits their later chances on the labour market. A key reason for the increasing scarcity on the student housing market is the growing number of international students.

### 6.2 The Collapse of Private Supply

The scarcity has increased significantly because total supply has decreased. Approximately 5,000 student rooms were added. But against this, the number of students living in private rental housing decreased by **17,800** in the past year. This shrinks the total number of available rooms. The supply in the 19 student cities is estimated at 322,400 student rooms—approximately **13,500 units lower** than in the 2023-24 academic year. The number of private student room sales continues to increase, and the peak of sales does not yet appear to have been reached. According to the Kadaster, both tightened regulations and the transfer tax, the introduction of purchase protection, the influence of parts of the Affordable Rent Act, and box 3 rules are reasons for sale.

### 6.3 The Fraternity Gatekeeper and the Hospiteren Filter

**This is the critical, underreported mechanism that distorts the statistics.** The *hospiteren* system is not a neutral allocation mechanism. It is a social filter that determines who has access to the affordable segment of the student housing market. DUWO, with over 33,000 rooms, has acknowledged that non-members of student associations have "far fewer chances" of obtaining a room. The SSH director has stated that the system "structurally excludes certain groups of students" and creates "increasing inequality".

The data confirms this. In Leiden, **40% of *hospiteer* rooms** are exclusively occupied by members of four fraternities that represent only 16% of the student population. The *hospiteren* system functions as a closed network: fraternity houses select their own members, and rejected non-members are left to the open market. One fraternity member described the process: "Usually you have a good chance that you'll get it... When a house doesn't see a match, the process doesn't stop there. Then they say: 'we don't see it happening', but they do think along with you... Then you get forwarded to other houses".

**The statistical consequence is profound.** The "average" rent figures reported by Kamernet and Nibud are derived from a market that includes a substantial volume of rooms allocated through fraternity networks at below-market or average prices. These rooms are not available to non-members. Therefore, the average is **structurally depressed** by a segment of the market that is inaccessible to the very students who are most in need of affordable housing.

A student who does not join a fraternity—whether by choice, exclusion, or inability to participate in *ontgroening* and the associated social rituals—is not a candidate for the average rent. They are forced into the **residual market**: private landlords, commercial platforms, and the highest-priced segment. They are not average cases; they are **forced high-price cases**.

The student who refuses to pay the compliance price—hazing, sexual ranking and coercion, participation in informal economies—is not making a lifestyle choice. They are accepting a **structural penalty**: exclusion from the affordable segment and forced entry into the expensive one. The statistics do not capture this penalty. They report an average that the non-participant never sees.

### 6.4 The International Student and the Hollowing of the Independent Category

International students are not the cause of the statistical distortion. They are fellow casualties of the same doctrine. The doctrine excludes them from the network rent; the legislation and policy enforce the exclusion; and the statistics then report their presence in the independent category as if it were a measure of the system's success.

Almost every international student is registered as *uitwonend* (living independently). Of the approximately 130,000 international students in the Netherlands, **94% are *uitwonend***. They must be. They have no parental home in the Netherlands to register at. The DUO system requires a registered address other than the parents' address to qualify for the higher grant; for international students, every address is other than their parents' address.

International students also face systematic exclusion from the fraternity network. One in three international students reports experiencing discrimination in housing, with advertisements explicitly stating "no internationals" or "Dutch only". More than half believe they pay higher rents than Dutch students, and research confirms they pay between **€70 and €85 more per month** for the same room.

This has a profound effect on the statistics. When CBS or Kences reports that "44% of students live independently," that figure includes international students who are *uitwonend* by definition and who pay a premium for their exclusion from the network. Remove them from the calculation, and the proportion of Dutch students living independently falls dramatically.

### 6.5 The Wealthy Dutch Independent: Parental Property as a Hidden Subsidy

The Dutch students who remain in the independent category are not a random sample. They are disproportionately those whose parents have the capital to purchase a home for them. Nibud has documented the growing dependence of students on parental support. In 2024, **57% of students received money from their parents**, with an average monthly contribution of **€225**. Students without a supplementary grant or with a low grant receive a higher direct parental contribution than students with a supplementary grant.

Crucially, the Tweede Kamer has noted that uitwonende HBO and WO students who do not borrow and receive no parental contribution or supplementary grant come up an average of **€532 per month short** to cover the minimum costs of study and living. The loan system did not create independence. It created a system in which parental wealth became the decisive variable in whether a student could live independently at all.

### 6.6 The Hidden Shortage: Students Who Have Given Up

The most damning statistical distortion is the "hidden shortage." Thousands of students have stopped searching for housing altogether because they consider the market hopeless. Kences director Reijnder Jan Spits has confirmed this mechanism: when inventory hits zero, students drop out of sight. Conversely, if new rooms are constructed, latent demand instantly spikes as inactive searchers re-enter the market. Kences states: "We see in new construction projects that students who had given up hope are actively searching again".

The National Student Union (LSVb) has estimated that there are currently around **23,000 student rooms too few**, and that 22,000 students could not find a room last autumn. The shortage could reach **63,000 by 2032**. A 2025 Kences monitor found that **43% of students living at home have stopped searching for rooms altogether because it is too expensive**. The hidden shortage is the statistical residue of a system that has failed so comprehensively that its victims no longer register as victims.

### 6.7 The Composite Effect: What the Statistics Actually Show

When these distortions are combined, the picture is clear. The statistic "44% of students live independently" does not mean that 44% of Dutch students have achieved independence through a functioning system. It means that:

- A large and growing proportion of the independent category consists of international students who are *uitwonend* by necessity and who pay a premium for their exclusion from the network. They are not the problem; their inclusion in the category is the symptom.
- A significant proportion of the remaining Dutch independent students are supported by parental wealth, often through property ownership that converts housing into an income stream.
- A substantial portion of the affordable housing supply is allocated through fraternity networks that are closed to non-members and gated by social compliance, depressing the reported "average" rent and concealing the true market price faced by outsiders.
- A smaller and shrinking proportion are genuinely independent Dutch students without parental support, and these students face structural deficits that force them into debt, precarious work, or both—while paying the non-network price.
- An unknown number of Dutch students have given up searching altogether and are invisible in the statistics.

The system does not produce independent students. It produces a statistical category called "independent students" that is filled by different populations depending on who can afford to enter it or who is granted access by the fraternity gatekeeper. The Dutch student without parental support and without fraternity membership is being priced out of the category—and the category is being filled by those whose independence is subsidised by wealth, by the absence of any alternative, or by network access purchased with compliance.

**The statistics are not a measurement error. They are an instrument of the exclusion.** They produce an image of a functioning system while concealing the mechanism that excludes those without family capital or social compliance.

## 7. The Childcare Benefits Scandal: Institutional Racism as Policy

The most documented evidence of enforced moral hierarchy is the *toeslagenschandaal*—the childcare benefits scandal. On 25 May 2022, the Dutch government publicly admitted for the first time that **institutional racism** in part of the Dutch Tax and Customs Administration was the root cause of the scandal. This admission followed the resignation of the Dutch government in 2021.

The mechanism was algorithmic. To create risk profiles of individuals applying for childcare benefits, the Tax and Customs Administration used algorithms in which **"foreign sounding names"** and **"dual nationality"** were used as indicators of potential fraud. The result was that thousands of racialised low- and middle-income families were subjected to scrutiny, falsely accused of fraud, and asked to repay benefits they had obtained completely legally.

Since 2005, thousands of innocent families suffered financial hardship due to the structure of the childcare allowance system. Regulations required that minor or even incomprehensible administrative mistakes could compel families to repay their annual received benefits in full, frequently amounting to tens of thousands of euros; amounts out of reach for many of them. The Tax and Customs Administration (Belastingdienst), incentivised to maximise revenue, conducted an expansive and proactive investigation into suspected fraud. This process led to erroneous benefit terminations, illegal blacklisting, systematic disregard of parental appeals and the subsequent out-of-home placement of **2,090 children**.

Subsequent exposés uncovered that the Dutch Tax and Customs Administration employed discriminatory profiling practices to hunt for new cases of suspected fraud, disproportionately targeting individuals with non-Dutch and/or immigrant backgrounds. These actions prompted allegations of systemic discrimination within the tax authority. Despite issued warnings in 2012 and 2014 regarding the severity of the Tax and Customs Administration's recovery policy, responsible officials failed to act and members of the cabinet did not inform Parliament about these urgent concerns.

Two ensuing parliamentary inquiries concluded that human rights were overlooked across all arms of government in an increasingly uncompromising political and social context. The reports determined that fundamental rights had been violated and the rule of law undermined. They further highlighted that the social security and allowance systems intended to aid citizens instead caused substantial harm and that the tax authority acted prejudicial. Concurrently, the Parliamentary Inquiry Committee on Fraud Policy and Service Provision identified **"institutional racism"** as a contributing factor.

This was not an error. It was the operation of a system that used racial and national markers as proxies for fraud. The state apparatus was deployed to discipline a specific population—those with migration backgrounds—while the moral narrative of Dutch honesty and law-abidingness remained intact. And for the students in these families, the effect was to destroy any possibility of upwards mobility. The parents' debt became the child's inheritance. The home was lost. The capital that might have supported a student through university was confiscated.

The mechanism of exclusion operates across generations. Excluding the parent excludes the child.

## 8. The DUO Algorithm: Disciplining Students by Background

The enforcement mechanism extends to education. The Dutch Education Executive Agency (DUO) used an algorithm between 2012 and 2023 to check whether students were abusing the grant for students living away from home. The Dutch Data Protection Authority concluded that this approach was **discriminatory and therefore unlawful**.

The algorithm assigned a "risk score" based on type of education, distance between addresses, and age. There was no objective justification for these criteria. Furthermore, the Minister of Education, Culture and Science acknowledged that the algorithm had **indirectly discriminated** students with a migration background. A follow-up investigation commissioned by the Ministry of Education revealed that **86% of students who objected to DUO's decisions had a non-European migration background**.

DUO calculated with an algorithm the risk that a student would commit fraud with the uitwonendenbeurs. DUO selected students for a home visit using these criteria: Education type: an MBO programme gave a higher risk score than an HBO or WO programme. Distance: a shorter distance between the student's residential address and that of the parent(s) gave a higher risk score. Age: a lower age of the student gave a higher risk score. With a higher risk score, a student could (after manual selection by DUO) more often face a check and a home visit from DUO.

The Dutch DPA estimated that between 2013 and 2022, DUO selected **21,500 students** and checked them for fraud, partly on the basis of the algorithm's calculations. Approximately **10,000 students** are now receiving compensation because the fraud investigations were based on discriminatory selection. The government has set aside **€61 million** to compensate all current and former students who were fined by DUO and had to pay back their basic student grant.

This is the mechanism in operation. The state identifies a population—students with migration backgrounds—and deploys its enforcement apparatus disproportionately against them. Meanwhile, the student in Scenario C, whose parents own property and generate rental income, is not subject to the same scrutiny. The algorithm does not flag wealth; it flags foreignness. Upwards mobility becomes not just difficult but dangerous—the higher you climb, the more likely you are to be investigated.

## 9. The Welfare Fraud Act: Criminalising the Powerless

While the state deploys algorithms to target racialised populations, it simultaneously enforces a punitive regime against welfare recipients. Hertogh (2024) argues that the Dutch Welfare Fraud Act of 2012 significantly intensified sanctions for "fraudulent" behaviour of welfare recipients. This Act introduced fines of up to **€50,000**, and made it possible for fraudsters to be entirely excluded from certain welfare services. Behaviours such as failing to disclose relevant information or refusing certain job offers were explicitly classified as prohibited.

Crucially, eligibility for benefits was not only conditional on compliance with these rules, but violations could trigger new punishments. Convicts of welfare fraud were not just punished by the obligation to reimburse earlier payments, but could also receive additional fines or even prison sentences. Beyond these formal penalties, the stigma of being labelled a "fraudster" often resulted in long-term exclusion from insurance, bank accounts, housing, and employment.

This culminated in the 2014 Fraud Act, which introduced a system of high sanction in all cases of benefit fraud, even if they were caused by administrative errors. Public debate was further fuelled by the life-changing severity of the punishments inflicted on the victims. Pieter Omtzigt, one of the members of parliament who brought the scandal to light, emphasised that beyond ethnic profiling and intensified welfare fraud investigations, two other practices also contributed to the scandal. First, there was an attitudinal issue amongst the tax inspectors such that even the smallest mistakes were interpreted as evidence of fraud and penalised with the maximum fines permitted by law. Ten Hooven (2021) reported that **something as minor as a missing signature could result in the confiscation of housing and financial assets**.

This relates to the second problem: the rigid reimbursement policy. Accused recipients were required to repay all childcare benefits in full and immediately without the usual legal proceedings.

This is the double standard in operation. The state uses algorithms that disproportionately target migration backgrounds for fraud investigation, while simultaneously imposing draconian penalties on those who receive welfare—the poorest and most vulnerable. The message is that fraud by the powerful is a business model; fraud by the powerless is a crime. The student in Scenario B, who faces a structural deficit, is a potential target. The student in Scenario C, whose parents' wealth generates income, is not.

## 10. Moral Superiority as Enforcement: The 7 Vinkjes and Calvinist Chosenness

The enforcement mechanism is not only legal and administrative. It is also cultural and moral. The Dutch self-image—constructed over centuries—positions the Netherlands as moderate, rational, and civilised. This self-image is rooted in Calvinism, which shaped the Dutch Republic and provided a framework in which Protestants saw themselves as the **chosen people**.

The contemporary expression of this moral hierarchy is the **"7 vinkjes"**—the seven checkmarks identified by journalist Joris Luyendijk. In his book, *De zeven Vinkjes – Hoe mannen zoals ik de baas spelen* ("The Seven Check Marks – How men like me play boss"), Luyendijk argues that it is high time that privileged people like him make room for people with fewer privileges. By "people like me", he means male (check), white (check), straight (check), who has at least one parent born in the Netherlands, at least one highly educated parent earning good money, a certain social status or cultural capital, and a university degree (check, check, check, check). People with seven check marks should make room for the ones who are not always given the benefit of the doubt because they are seen as the Other.

Luyendijk's list of check marks is incomplete. His book fails to mention several privileges that significantly affect a person's opportunities and position. For instance: Being cisgender, being able-bodied and without chronic illnesses, having no discernable religion. People with seven check marks who are transgender, use a wheelchair, can't work for more than five hours a day or always wear a yarmulke will still have fewer opportunities to "play boss".

The 7 vinkjes are not a causal mechanism. They are a heuristic — a list of markers that tend to co-occur among those who occupy positions of power. The list's function is to make visible a pattern of privilege that is otherwise difficult to see. It is not a measured variable, and it does not explain how the privilege is reproduced. The mechanisms that reproduce the privilege are the ones this paper documents elsewhere: the compliance continuum, the fraternity gatekeeper, the kruiwagen network, the welfare fraud legislation, the DUO algorithm. The 7 vinkjes name the pattern. The doctrine produces it. The legislation and policy enforce it.

The 7 vinkjes are not merely a description of privilege. They are the operating system of the moral hierarchy. Those who possess the markers Luyendijk identifies are disproportionately likely to occupy positions where their social capital secures their standing. Those who lack the markers are disproportionately likely to face the structural disadvantages the paper documents. The student in Scenario C likely possesses most or all of the 7 vinkjes. The student in Scenario B likely does not.

The moral hierarchy converts structural exclusion into personal failure. The excluded student is not told that the system is designed to exclude them — designed through legislation and policy brought forth from doctrine. They are told that they did not work hard enough, did not plan well enough, did not choose the right programme. The moral narrative is the final instrument of exclusion: it makes the excluded complicit in their own exclusion by convincing them it was their choice.

The same logic applies to the fraternity gatekeeper. The student who is excluded from the network rent is not told that the network is gated by compliance. They are told that they simply did not find a room. The compliance price—the hazing, the ranking, the informal economies—is not presented as a price. It is presented as "student life," as "tradition," as "belonging." The student who declines to pay it is not told that they have declined to pay it. They are told that they failed to secure housing.

## 11. Kruiwagen Culture: The Labour Market as a Closed Network

The moral hierarchy is reinforced by the **kruiwagen** culture—the Dutch term for getting a job or privilege through connections. Dutch labour market experts keep talking about a "matching problem." But the real problem is one rarely named: **Kruiwagen culture**. Jobs arranged through closed circles, quiet referrals, and opportunities handed "onder het tafel" (under the table). Call it what it is: **NEPOTISM dressed up as normal hiring**.

The *kruiwagen* is a wheelbarrow, but in its pejorative sense it refers to someone who helps another obtain a position or privilege. In the Dutch labour market, this means that access to jobs—especially desirable ones—is mediated through networks that are closed to outsiders. Those inside the network can afford to be honest because their position is secured. Those outside must find other means to survive.

For many—women of colour, African professionals, internationals—the barrier isn't skill; it's access. You're filtered out before a human even looks at your CV. HR systems reward familiarity, not capability. Companies shout about staff shortages while ignoring qualified candidates who don't fit the "inner circle" of "7 vinkjes" who might be upstaged by internationals professionals who might have better qualifications. Insiders move job to job, while others are told there's a "skills mismatch." And layered on top is the "7 vinkjes" effect—the well-documented Dutch privilege template. **If you match all seven, doors open. If you don't, the system quietly closes ranks**.

The combination of the *kruiwagen* and the 7 vinkjes produces a system where the established class can afford to be honest because the system is designed — through legislation and policy brought forth from doctrine — to protect them, while the working class is left to compete for scraps. The student in Scenario C inherits the *kruiwagen*. The student in Scenario B must build it from nothing, and when they fail, they are blamed for the failure.

The exclusion is complete: the student without family capital is excluded from housing (by rent and the fraternity gatekeeper), from stability (by deficit), from honest opportunity (by closed networks). Each instrument reinforces the others.

## 12. Discussion: The Mechanism Is Designed — Through Doctrine, Legislation, and Policy

The evidence presented in this paper supports the proposition that the mechanism is not accidental—it is designed. The design operates through:

3. **The fraternity and *hospiteren* gatekeeper** that segments the housing market, granting affordable access to network members who pay the compliance price—hazing, sexual ranking and coercion, participation in informal economies—while forcing non-members into the highest-price segment, and thereby depressing the reported "average" rent that conceals this penalty.
4. **Algorithmic profiling** that disproportionately targets racialised populations for fraud investigation.
5. **Punitive welfare legislation** that criminalises administrative error and imposes devastating sanctions on the poorest and most vulnerable.
6. **Moral hierarchy** rooted in Calvinist notions of chosenness that legitimises the exclusion of outsiders and positions the Dutch elite as morally superior.
7. **Kruiwagen culture** that allocates jobs through closed networks, ensuring that the established class maintains its position while outsiders are left to compete for scraps.

These instruments are not separate. They are interconnected. The grant structure creates the deficit. The housing market and fraternity gatekeeper convert the deficit into exclusion while concealing it in the statistics. The algorithmic profiling identifies who is suspect. The welfare legislation determines who is punished. The moral hierarchy justifies the differential treatment. The *kruiwagen* ensures that those who are not suspect are rewarded with jobs and security.

**It is the system functioning as designed — through legislation and policy brought forth from doctrine — to exclude those without family capital or network access.**

The self-referential dimension of this analysis is that it emerged from a first-person account of navigating this system. The account described a student who received €1,000 from DUO, paid €900 in rent—a non-network price—and was left with €100 for food and all other expenses. But the €100 was not the full measure of the deficit. Measured against the Nibud minimum budget of €1,723 per month (excluding tuition), the student faced a **monthly deficit of €723**. The student bridged this gap through loans and work—because the formal system provided no path. When the student refused to participate in the only opportunities available, they were left without a degree and without a path.

The evidence confirms that this is not an isolated experience. It is the predictable outcome of a system that enforces differential treatment through legal, administrative, cultural, and statistical mechanisms. The three scenarios demonstrate the structural asymmetry. The student in Scenario B is not failing because of personal deficiency. They are failing because the system is designed for Scenario C—and because the statistics that claim to describe their situation are derived from a market they cannot access.

**There is no upwards mobility for the student without parental support (a home) and without fraternity network access. It is the system functioning as designed to exclude those without family capital or social compliance.**

The system does not provide a ladder. It provides a sieve. Those with family capital or network access pass through; those without are filtered out. The enforcement apparatus ensures that the filter is not just economic but also legal, administrative, moral, and statistical. The student without parental support is not just poor; they are suspect. They are not just struggling; they are excluded by design.

The statistics that purport to measure the system's functioning are themselves instruments of this exclusion. By including international students, wealthy independents, and fraternity members in the "independent student" category, they produce an image of a system that works for a majority, while concealing the structural devastation experienced by the minority who are genuinely independent, genuinely without support, and genuinely outside the network.

## 13. Conclusion

The Dutch state operates a system of enforcement that is not neutral. It targets certain populations for scrutiny while protecting others. # Appendix A: Prey or Predator — Coercion as the Currency of Authority

## A.0 Method and Thesis

### A.0.1 Method

This appendix is a case study. It takes a single first-person account and reads it through established sociological and theoretical frameworks to develop concepts that other researchers can test against additional cases.

The account is treated as a case that illustrates mechanisms the main paper documents structurally. The main paper establishes, through institutional sources, that the Dutch system operates a form of credentialed authority that excludes those who have not purchased it. This appendix shows what that mechanism looks like when it is experienced at the micro level — in a voice, in a flinch, in a rebuke, in a four-stage sequence of invitation, neglect, intervention, and punishment.

Case studies are a legitimate method. They can show mechanisms in operation, trace sequences, and capture the texture of interactions that statistical data flattens. The account is corroborated by external sources — voice-perception research, first-person accounts from forums, published workplace studies, Dutch journalism on internship exploitation, and the perpetrator-predation literature — which establish that the incidents the subject reports are instances of documented patterns, not idiosyncratic events. The limits of the method are acknowledged in §A.16, which specifies what would count against the appendix's readings.

### A.0.2 Thesis

The thesis of this appendix is:

> The Dutch system operates a form of legitimate authority — **coercion authority** — that is granted only to those who have paid for it by participating in, and then administering, coercion. This authority is defended through deniable micro-interactions that penalise those who bypass it. The account documents three forms of bypass — **unmediated presence**, **uncredentialed merit**, and **remediation** — and three corresponding penalties. The penalties reveal that the credential is not the source of authority, not the source of value, and not even the mechanism by which duty is discharged. The credential is a fiction maintained by exclusion. The system does not want upward mobility for those without it. It wants compliance. And when compliance is not forthcoming, it wants exclusion.

This thesis is stated here in full so that the reader can hold it in mind throughout. The rest of the appendix is its development and defence.

### A.0.3 The Account

The account is from a male perspective. It does not speak for women, for other marginalised groups, or for all Dutch people. It is one case, analysed for its conceptual yield. The subject reports the events in good faith; the appendix interprets them in good faith. Where interpretations compete, the appendix prefers the reading with the strongest support and states the alternatives in §A.16.

## A.1 The Account

The account describes a man who entered Dutch education and employment, performed at a high level, encountered sustained social and professional resistance, refused to be broken by it, and was subsequently invited into positions that would have required him to administer the exploitation of others. He refused those positions as well, and was left outside both the rewards and the protections of the system he had navigated.

### A.1.1 The Voice

The subject has a deep masculine voice. He reports that it is the first thing people notice about him and the last thing they forget. When he speaks in a group, the room often goes quiet — not because he has said anything remarkable, but because the voice itself commands attention. Strangers sometimes ask about it. Some people smile nervously. Others lean back slightly. A few have told him, unprompted, that he should consider a career in radio.

The voice is not a performance. It is not something he can turn off. It is present in every interaction, audible to everyone in the room. And it is the trigger for the incidents that follow.

This is consistent with the voice-perception literature. Puts et al. (2016) demonstrated that low-pitched male voices evolved primarily to intimidate male rivals, not to attract mates. The retaliation-cost model (ScienceDirect, 2020) shows that low-pitched male voices "make male speakers sound more dominant, intimidating, threatening, and likely to aggress" and that men with higher threat potential have elevated recognition accuracy for such voices. A LinkedIn post by image consultant Natalie Wong lists "INTIMIDATION" as the first negative effect of a deep voice: "While a deep voice can inspire respect, it can also be perceived as intimidating or distant."

The subject's experience is not idiosyncratic. A Reddit thread in r/Stoicism titled "You always sound angry or defensive, when you speak..." describes the same dynamic: "My answers to questions are direct and to the point... I pause, determine my reply thoughtfully, say it.. and yet again it happens... I have a deep voice... It's almost like i am being gaslighted." A social anxiety forum thread has a user reporting: "Through out the years i have been mocked and criticized about my deep voice... The thing that hurts me the most is that people say my voice is scary... Usually people asked me to repeat myself because they cannot understand what i am saying with my deep voice which has affected my self confidence overall."

### A.1.2 The Embodied Penalty

Before any test of character, the subject's physical and vocal presence produces exclusion.

**The scream.** In one encounter, a woman screamed at the subject that she did not condone the patriarchy. He had not spoken about gender, politics, or power. He had not spoken at all. The scream was triggered by his presence — specifically, by his deep masculine voice. The statement was not a response to anything he said. It was a response to what his voice is.

**The repetition-and-turn.** In group settings, men would ask the subject to repeat a statement he had made, then turn to another person and respond to them instead, treating the subject's contribution as if it had not occurred. The repetition was demanded not because he was unclear, but because the refusal to understand functioned as a way of withholding recognition.

**Picking him last in teamwork.** In team assignments, the subject was routinely chosen last. The team assignment is a miniature of the labour market: the subject was present, competent, and available, but he was chosen only when no alternative remained.

**The flinch-and-recover.** Interlocutors would physically recoil — a slight lean back, a tightening of the face, a hand raised as if to stop traffic — before recovering composure and responding as though nothing had happened. The recoil was the tell. The recovery was the deniability.

**The hostile tone audit.** The subject reports speaking calmly and at normal volume, and being told: "There's no need to be aggressive." The aggression existed only in the ear of the listener. The deep masculine voice registered as aggression regardless of the intent behind it.

**The "you're making people uncomfortable" report.** The subject was informed, often through a third party, that his presence made others uncomfortable. No specific behaviour was named. No incident was cited. The discomfort was attributed to him as a fact, as though it were a property of his body rather than a response to it.

**The mockery of physical strength.** In a recurring pattern across years, women mocked the subject's physical strength and fitness — his broad chest and his training — in what he experienced as an attempt to depress him and undermine his physical self-regard. The mockery was not provoked by any comment or action on his part.

### A.1.3 The Demoralisation Attempt

Across roughly his early-to-late twenties, the subject encountered sustained pressure aimed at breaking him down.

The mockery of his physical strength was one instrument. Another was the attack on his status. After he had produced what he reports as the best performance at his company, his employer responded by mocking his education: "You have a VWO diploma, but you never finished university." The message was not about performance but about belonging: you may perform, but you do not belong.

He continued to behave gently toward women and children, and reports that innocent women and children continued to like him, but the system rewarded neither the gentleness nor the endurance nor the excellence.

The demoralisation attempt is a filter. Those who break become available for exploitation. Those who do not break become a problem.

The subject did not break. This is the pivot of the account.

### A.1.4 The Recruitment Attempt

Once demoralisation had failed, the system changed strategy. It offered the subject a role.

He was offered managerial positions that would have placed him above others in the hierarchy. He was invited to join what he calls the "predator crew" — the informal layer of supervisors, team leads, and small operators who administer extraction at ground level. In one offer, he was asked to manage mentally weak people and young people from broken homes, and to see it as charity to let them work for pennies. In another, he was offered a position at a call centre where his role would be to use his strong character to attract interns to work for free.

The subject refused. He was left without a degree, without a path, and without upward mobility.

### A.1.5 The Concept: Coercion Authority

The concept developed here to name the mechanism running through these three elements is **coercion authority**: the form of legitimate authority that is granted only to those who have paid for it by participating in, and then administering, coercion.

Coercion authority is a species of credentialism in Randall Collins's (1979) sense. Collins argued that credentials are not primarily measures of skill; they are cultural currency that groups use to monopolize access to positions. Coercion authority specifies how this particular credential is acquired — through the endurance and administration of coercion — and how it is defended — through deniable micro-interactions that penalise those who bypass it.

The concept also occupies a distinct position in Max Weber's (1922/1978) typology of authority. It is not traditional (inherited), not charismatic (personal magnetism), and not legal-rational (rule-based office). It is a fourth type: authority acquired through surviving degradation and maintained through administering it.

The account documents two forms of authority that bypass this credential. Both are treated as illegitimate by the arrangement:

- **Unmediated authority**: innate, embodied, present without action. The deep voice, the broad frame. It commands attention without having paid.
- **Uncredentialed merit**: earned, performed, demonstrated through action. The highest achievement, the duties beyond the job description. It produces value without having paid.

A third form emerges from the account's remediation sequence, described in §A.7.2: the demonstration that the credential is not even the mechanism by which duty is discharged.

## A.2 Theoretical Context

Coercion authority overlaps with several established literatures, and its distinctiveness is best appreciated by comparison.

**Social closure.** Frank Parkin (1979) distinguished between exclusionary closure (dominant groups restricting access to resources) and usurpatory closure (subordinate groups challenging exclusion). Credential defence is exclusionary closure, but operating through a specific mechanism: deniable micro-interactions rather than formal rules. What coercion authority adds is the content of the closure: the credential is purchased through coercion, and its defence is the punishment of those who demonstrate that the purchase was unnecessary.

**Professional jurisdiction.** Andrew Abbott (1988) argued that professions are defined by their jurisdiction over tasks, and that jurisdictional boundaries are maintained through negotiation and conflict. The "that's not your job" rebuke is a jurisdictional claim — it asserts that the task belongs to the credentialled person, regardless of whether the credentialled person has performed it. What coercion authority adds is the observation that jurisdictional claims activate selectively: they are dormant when the credentialled person neglects the task, and active when an unauthorised person performs it.

**Hazing and initiation.** The literature on hazing (van Gennep, 1909/1960; Turner, 1969; Nuwer, 2000; Keating et al., 2005) documents how degradation is used to produce solidarity and hierarchy. Coercion authority adds a structural dimension: the degradation is not only a rite of belonging but a purchase of the right to exclude others. The purchase price is the credential.

**Street-level bureaucracy.** Michael Lipsky (1980) documented how frontline workers exercise discretion in implementing policy, and how they use that discretion to manage their own working conditions. The predator crew (§A.13) is a variant: middle-layer operators who administer extraction on behalf of the system, and who extract their own privileges in the process.

**Perpetrator predation.** A Cambridge Core article proposes "perpetrator predation" as a framework: "a 'predator' is a person who 'lives by predation,' or 'looks for other people in order to use, control, or harm them in some way'". The framework "draws our attention to the individual who targets those characteristics" and "brings power into the picture, with power disparities often separating predator from prey". It notes that "potential aggressors choose targets strategically, focusing their hostility on people who seem difficult to like and/or those who appear to be vulnerable and unable to defend themselves". This framework grounds the predator crew concept.

**Symbolic violence.** Pierre Bourdieu (1991) described violence that cannot be named without the sufferer appearing paranoid, aggressive, or entitled. The deniable techniques described in §A.11 are the mechanism through which coercion authority is defended without being named.

These literatures do not explain coercion authority, but they situate it. The contribution of this appendix is to name a specific form of credentialed authority — one whose currency is coercion — and to describe the micro-interactions through which it is defended.

## A.3 The Trial of Endurance and the Purchase of Authority

The main paper traced the Dutch moral hierarchy to a Calvinist inheritance in which election is demonstrated by endurance: the world is a test, and those who remain steadfast under suffering are marked as chosen. Max Weber (1905/2002) documented how this theology became a this-worldly discipline in which worldly standing became a sign of grace, while failure and collapse became signs of damnation. Simon Schama (1987) documented how the resulting self-image — sober, disciplined, morally superior — justified both prosperity and exclusion.

That inheritance supplies the form of authority in the system: authority is not inherent, it is demonstrated. But the account suggests that the content of the demonstration has changed. In the inherited doctrine, one demonstrates election by enduring suffering. In the account, one demonstrates standing by enduring coercion and then administering it. Endurance is still the price, but what one endures, and what one is licensed to do afterwards, is coercion itself.

This is what the concept of coercion authority captures. The person who has been hazed holds a credential. The person who has managed the vulnerable holds a credential. The credential is real, but its currency is coercion — and it retains its value only as long as everyone agrees that no other currency exists.

The doctrine has a corollary that the account makes explicit. If authority must be purchased, then authority that was not purchased is illegitimate regardless of its source. There are three such illegitimate sources, and the account documents all three: authority that is innate (the subject's voice and frame), authority that is earned through unauthorised merit (the subject's record of highest achievement), and authority that is exercised through remediation (the subject's discharge of a duty the credentialled assignee neglected). The first is illegitimate because it was never purchased. The second is illegitimate because it was purchased with the wrong currency — with work, rather than with the credential. The third is illegitimate because it bypasses the credential entirely, demonstrating that the credential is not the mechanism by which duty is discharged. All three expose the arrangement.

### A.3.1 The Credential as Composite

The credential is not a single thing. It is a composite of three elements, each of which must be acquired:

**Social markers.** The "7 vinkjes" described in the main paper: having at least one highly educated or wealthy parent, at least one parent born in the Netherlands, being male, heterosexual, white, having attended gymnasium or VWO, and holding a university degree. These markers are not skills; they are signals of belonging. They indicate that the holder is the kind of person the arrangement recognises as legitimate.

**Network access.** The *kruiwagen* system: jobs arranged through closed circles, quiet referrals, and opportunities handed "onder het tafel." The main paper documents this structurally; the appendix shows what it looks like when someone lacks it.

**Demonstrated willingness to endure and then administer coercion.** The compliance continuum described in §A.8: hazing, sexual ranking and coercion, participation in the informal economies of the house. This is the element that cannot be acquired by accident or by birth. It must be performed. It must be chosen. And it must be repeated.

The three elements interlock. Social markers open the door to the network. The network provides access to the compliance continuum. The compliance continuum completes the credential. The person who holds the full credential has paid for it in all three currencies. The person who lacks any one element is incomplete.

The subject possessed the first two elements in part — he is male, Dutch, and capable of network participation. But he refused the third. He would not participate in the compliance continuum. He would not be hazed. He would not administer coercion to others. He would not pay the price that would have completed his credential.

This is why he was excluded despite his authority and his merit. The credential is not a reward for authority or merit. It is a reward for compliance. The subject had the first two elements but not the third. He was therefore incomplete. And incompleteness is treated as illegitimacy.

### A.3.2 The Compliance Continuum

The compliance continuum runs from the routine to the extreme. At each stage, the person is asked to demonstrate willingness to participate in the arrangement. At each stage, the demand normalises the next.

**Routine compliance.** *Ontgroening*: the hazing period that new members of Dutch student associations must endure. Documented practices include forced alcohol consumption, physical exhaustion, sleep deprivation, humiliation, degradation, and the performance of degrading acts. The function is not initiation in any benign sense. It is a filter. It selects for those willing to endure humiliation in exchange for belonging.

**Sexual compliance.** The *bangalijst*: sexual ranking lists that circulate within fraternities, ranking female members and guests by sexual attractiveness and, in some cases, by sexual availability. The main paper documents these scandals structurally. The appendix notes their function in the compliance continuum: they are a demand that women participate in and remain silent about the sexual coercion that legitimises men's credentials.

**Criminal compliance.** Participation in the informal economies of the house, including documented drug distribution. The compliance continuum does not end at hazing or sexual ranking. It continues to the point where the person has participated in activities that cannot be disclosed, that cannot be undone, and that bind them permanently to the arrangement.

The subject refused to enter the continuum at any level. He did not pay the first price. He was therefore excluded from the network that the continuum gated. And he was forced into the residual market, where he paid the non-network price.

## A.4 The Two Forms of Unauthorised Authority

The account documents two distinct forms of authority that the subject possesses and that the system treats as illegitimate.

### A.4.1 Unmediated Authority

Unmediated authority is the authority of presence. The subject's voice and frame carry weight in any room they enter. This authority was never granted by any institution. It was never earned through any process. It is simply present, in every interaction, audible and visible to everyone. It commands attention without having paid for the right to do so.

The analytical point is that the deep masculine voice is a form of authority that the system did not grant and cannot revoke. Bourdieu's (1977) concept of *hexis* — the bodily disposition that communicates social position without speech — is useful here, but with an inversion. Bourdieu described hexis as the embodiment of acquired position, internalised through practice and turned into permanent dispositions. The subject's voice is not acquired. It is innate, present in every interaction, and audible to everyone in the room. It is therefore **unmediated capital**: authority that arrived without having been purchased.

If authority in the system is supposed to be earned through the coercion credential, then unmediated authority is a problem. It commands attention without having paid. It carries weight without a membership behind it. And because it is audible, everyone present can observe that the credential is not the only source of authority in the room.

### A.4.2 Uncredentialed Merit

Uncredentialed merit is the authority of achievement. The subject reports that he performs at the highest level in his employment, that he enacts the role of highest achiever, and that he fulfils duties beyond his job description — duties that no one asked of him and that no credential authorised him to assume. He does not merely meet expectations; he exceeds them, repeatedly and visibly. And he continues to do so even when the exceeding is not rewarded.

This form of authority is often more threatening than unmediated presence. A person whose voice carries weight may simply be a curiosity — an exception to be managed. But a person who consistently outperforms, who does more than the role requires, and who does so without having paid into the credential system is not an exception. He is a demonstration. His work shows that the credential is not what produces value. His initiative shows that the role's boundaries are not necessary. His continued excellence, maintained without reward, shows that the arrangement's claim to be the only path to standing is false. This is not a person who can be ignored. This is a person whose existence is an argument.

The exclusion of high performers is a documented organizational dynamic. Rutgers University research published in the *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology* found that "teams with high rates of envy often ostracize their best performers, in turn leading those standout employees to sabotage productivity". The study confirms that "when high-performing employees – good soldiers – are excluded or undervalued by their peers, their motivation can shift from driving success to undermining it". A LinkedIn post on the "Tall Poppy Trap" describes the pattern: "In insecure cultures, the most talented people get targeted first. Their wins trigger social comparison; rivals then spread negative gossip, half-truths, and 'concerned' whispers to make them look risky. The goal isn't truth—it's to poison perception until opportunities dry up".

### A.4.3 The Third Demonstration: Remediation

A third form of unauthorised authority emerges from the account's remediation sequence, described in §A.7.2. The remediation sequence demonstrates that the credential is not even the mechanism by which duty is discharged. The assignee held the credential and did not act. The subject held no credential and acted. The stakeholders were served by the unauthorised party and harmed by the authorised one. The rebuke — "that wasn't your job" — is the arrangement's only available defence against a demonstration that the credential is functionally irrelevant to the work it claims to certify.

## A.5 The Trial of Endurance and the Penalty

The penalty must be analysed separately from the demoralisation that follows, because it operates on different logic. Demoralisation is a test: it assumes the subject is a candidate for classification. The penalty for unauthorised authority is pre-classification: it excludes before the test can be applied.

### A.5.1 The Three Demonstrations as a Unified Threat

The three demonstrations — presence, performance, and remediation — are not separate. They are a unified threat to the arrangement. Each demonstration chips away at the credential's legitimacy. Together, they expose it as arbitrary.

**Presence** shows that authority can exist without the credential. If a man can command a room without having been hazed, then the hazing was never necessary.

**Performance** shows that value can be produced without the credential. If a man can produce value, lead, take initiative, and fulfil duties beyond his role without the credential, then the credential is not what produces value.

**Remediation** shows that duty can be discharged without the credential. If a man can step in, after patience, and discharge a duty that the credentialled assignee neglected — and do so without claiming the credit — then the credential is not even the mechanism by which duty is discharged.

All three demonstrations lead to the same conclusion. If the credential is arbitrary, then the entire arrangement — the initiations, the rankings, the informal economies of the house, the managed exploitation of the vulnerable — is exposed as an arrangement maintained for the benefit of those who paid into it.

### A.5.2 The Arrangement's Fear

The arrangement's deepest fear is captured by a chain of reasoning that the account makes explicit:

> If a man can command a room without having been hazed, then the hazing was never necessary. If the hazing was never necessary, then the credential it confers is arbitrary. If the credential is arbitrary, then the entire arrangement — the initiations, the rankings, the informal economies of the house, the managed exploitation of the vulnerable — is exposed as an arrangement maintained for the benefit of those who paid into it.

This chain explains why the penalty is so severe, why it is deniable, and why it is administered by those who have paid into the arrangement.

The chain also explains the recruitment attempt. If the arrangement cannot defeat the demonstration, it can absorb it. The recruitment offer is the arrangement's attempt to convert evidence of the credential's irrelevance into a credential. The subject would no longer be the man who produced value without the credential. He would be the man who was granted the credential by the arrangement. The demonstration would be neutralised, not by being disproved, but by being incorporated.

### A.5.3 The System Wants Compliance, Not Mobility

The arrangement does not merely fail to help those without credentials. It actively penalises those who demonstrate that the credential is unnecessary. The penalty is not incidental; it is the mechanism through which compliance is enforced.

The system does not want upward mobility for those without parental support or network access. It wants compliance. And when compliance is not forthcoming, it wants exclusion.

This is the appendix's strongest structural claim. The system is not indifferent to those without credentials; it is hostile to them. The hostility is not personal; it is structural. The system cannot tolerate demonstrations that the credential is unnecessary, because those demonstrations threaten the legitimacy of the arrangement. So the system penalises the demonstrations. It excludes the demonstrators.

## A.6 The Penalty for Unmediated Presence

### A.6.1 The Lead Case: The Scream

In one encounter, a woman screamed at the subject that she did not condone the patriarchy. He had not spoken about gender, politics, or power. He had not spoken at all. The scream was triggered by his presence — specifically, by his deep masculine voice. The statement was not a response to anything he said. It was a response to what his voice is.

This is the cleanest evidence in the account for the embodied penalty. The scream cannot be a response to anything the subject said, because he said nothing. It cannot be a response to anything he did, because he had done nothing. It must be a response to his presence. The scream is the arrangement's defence against a presence that threatens its monopoly on authority.

The scream is not a contribution to a conversation. It is a categorical rejection of the subject's presence, performed at volume, in public, with the implicit expectation that others will understand and sympathise. And because the scream is framed as a response to patriarchy, the subject cannot object to it without appearing to be the very thing the scream denounces.

### A.6.2 Supporting Cases

**The repetition-and-turn.** In group settings, men would ask the subject to repeat a statement he had made, then turn to another person and respond to them instead. The repetition was demanded not because he was unclear, but because the refusal to understand functioned as a way of withholding recognition. The turn to another person completed the exclusion.

**The flinch-and-recover.** Interlocutors would physically recoil — a slight lean back, a tightening of the face, a hand raised as if to stop traffic — before recovering composure and responding as though nothing had happened. The recoil was the tell. The recovery was the deniability.

**The hostile tone audit.** The subject reports speaking calmly and at normal volume, and being told: "There's no need to be aggressive." This is a documented workplace dynamic. Amy Kean, writing on LinkedIn, describes being told by "privately educated middle class white women" that they "didn't like my tone" when delivering constructive feedback. Kean names the mechanism: "When someone says 'I don't like your tone' normally what they mean is that your words make them uncomfortable and they have no defence. Normally what they're doing is using THE WAY YOU SOUND... to actively shut you up, and declare your voice unacceptable". Kean cites Fricker's (2007) concept of "testimonial injustice" — when credibility is reduced because of voice and identity.

**The "you're making people uncomfortable" report.** The subject was informed, often through a third party, that his presence made others uncomfortable. No specific behaviour was named. No incident was cited. The discomfort was attributed to him as a fact, as though it were a property of his body rather than a response to it.

**The mockery of physical strength.** In a recurring pattern across years, women mocked the subject's physical strength and fitness — his broad chest and his training — in what he experienced as an attempt to depress him and undermine his physical self-regard.

### A.6.3 Interpretation

Each of these forms shares a structure. The subject's presence — not his words, not his actions, not his intentions — is treated as a statement. The statement is then attributed to him as an ideology, and the ideology is condemned. The condemnation is deniable because the ideology was never actually expressed. The subject is excluded, and the exclusion is framed as a principled response to something he never said.

The penalty operates by converting presence into ideology. A deep voice becomes a political position. A broad frame becomes a threat. A calm tone becomes aggression. The conversion is not a misunderstanding; it is a translation, performed so that the presence can be condemned without its bearer being able to answer.

The subject's experience is not idiosyncratic. A Reddit thread in r/Stoicism describes the same dynamic: "My answers to questions are direct and to the point... I pause, determine my reply thoughtfully, say it.. and yet again it happens... I have a deep voice... It's almost like i am being gaslighted." A social anxiety forum thread has a user reporting: "Through out the years i have been mocked and criticized about my deep voice... The thing that hurts me the most is that people say my voice is scary... Usually people asked me to repeat myself because they cannot understand what i am saying with my deep voice which has affected my self confidence overall."

## A.7 The Penalty for Uncredentialed Merit

### A.7.1 The Status Rebuke

After the subject had produced what he reports as the best performance at his company, his employer responded by mocking his education: "You have a VWO diploma, but you never finished university." The rebuke did not address the performance. It addressed the credential. It was delivered at the moment of strongest performance, precisely because that is the moment when the credential's irrelevance was most visible.

### A.7.2 The Lead Case: The Remediation Sequence

The most diagnostic sequence in the account unfolds in four stages.

**Stage one: the patient invitation.** A duty existed. A person had been assigned to fulfil it. Before any intervention by the subject, that person was invited — patiently and in a friendly manner — to take up the role and discharge the duty. The invitation was extended with the explicit purpose of allowing the assigned person to fulfil his responsibility and to claim the credit that fulfilment would bring.

**Stage two: the neglect.** The assigned person did not fulfil the duty. The role went unfilled. Stakeholders — the people the role existed to serve — suffered from the neglect. The harm was real, it was attributable, and it was ongoing.

**Stage three: the intervention after patience.** Only after the invitation had been made and the neglect had persisted did the subject step in. He performed the duty. The value was realised. The harm to stakeholders was arrested. And — critically — he did not claim the credit. He offered the assigned person the opportunity to take the appropriate honour for work that had, in fact, been done by the subject. The offer was declined.

**Stage four: the rebuke.** The response to the subject's intervention was: "That wasn't your job."

The rebuke is technically accurate. The duties were not assigned to the subject. And that accuracy is precisely what makes the rebuke useful to the arrangement. It invokes a rule — respect the boundaries of the assigned role — that the assigned person had already violated by neglect. The rule is applied to the subject alone.

This is the diagnostic point. **If the rule protected accountability, it would have been applied at stage two.** A rule that says duties belong to their assignee, and that others should not usurp them, must condemn the assignee who fails to perform those duties. It must name the neglect. It must acknowledge the harm to stakeholders. And it must credit the person who, having waited, repaired the harm without claiming the reward.

None of that happened. The rule was dormant through the neglect and the harm. It activated only at the moment the subject produced value that the credential had not authorised him to produce. **The rule is not a rule of role integrity. It is a rule of credential defence.** It exists to ensure that unauthorised competence is never permitted to enter the record — not because competence outside the role is harmful, but because competence outside the role is evidence.

The remediation sequence demonstrates a third form of unauthorised authority, distinct from presence and performance. Presence shows that authority can exist without the credential. Performance shows that value can be produced without the credential. Remediation shows that the credential is not even the mechanism by which duty is discharged. The assignee held the credential and did not act. The subject held no credential and acted. The stakeholders were served by the unauthorised party and harmed by the authorised one. The rebuke is the arrangement's only available defence against a demonstration that the credential is functionally irrelevant to the work it claims to certify.

### A.7.3 Supporting Cases

**The credit transfer.** The subject reports that his work was presented by another as their own. The mechanism removed the demonstration from the subject's record. If the work was credited to the credentialled person, the work no longer showed that the credential was unnecessary. It showed the opposite — it showed the credentialled person producing value, which is the arrangement's preferred narrative.

**The post-performance freeze-out.** After a period of unusually strong performance, the subject was quietly removed from the projects where he had performed. He was not told why. He was not told that anything had changed. The invitations simply stopped.

**The "you make others look bad" report.** The subject was informed, usually through a third party, that his performance was causing discomfort among colleagues. No specific incident was cited. No remedy was proposed.

**The redefinition of excellence as a character flaw.** The subject's conscientiousness was reframed as rigidity. His initiative was reframed as overreach. His thoroughness was reframed as an inability to prioritise. His willingness to do more was reframed as a failure to know his place.

**The recruitment to exploitation.** In one offer, the subject was asked to manage mentally weak people and young people from broken homes, and to see it as charity to let them work for pennies. In another, he was offered a position at a call centre where his role would be to use his strong character to attract interns to work for free.

### A.7.4 Interpretation

Each of these forms shares a structure. The subject's performance — not his intentions, not the content of his work — is treated as an accusation. The accusation is then either denied, reframed, or absorbed. The denial is deniable because the accusation was never explicitly made. The subject is excluded, and the exclusion is framed as a principled response to something he never did wrong.

Abbott's (1988) concept of professional jurisdiction is useful here. The "that's not your job" rebuke is a jurisdictional claim: it asserts that the task belongs to the credentialled person, regardless of whether the credentialled person has performed it. The rebuke is not about the task; it is about who is authorised to perform it.

## A.8 The Structural Function of the Penalty

The penalty is not a personal reaction to a threatening individual. It is a defence of an arrangement. The interlocutor or the colleague has no personal stake in the encounter and nothing to fear from the subject. What is threatened is the structure that grants them their standing.

That structure is the **coercion authority**: the arrangement in which legitimate authority is granted only to those who have paid for it by participating in, and then administering, coercion. The penalty is a defence of that arrangement against three demonstrations that the credential is not necessary.

All three demonstrations lead to the same conclusion. If the credential is arbitrary, then the entire arrangement — the initiations, the rankings, the informal economies of the house, the managed exploitation of the vulnerable — is exposed as an arrangement maintained for the benefit of those who paid into it.

None of these demonstrations is an attack. All are evidence. And evidence that the credential is counterfeit cannot be answered by argument, because the argument would require naming the credential — which is the one thing the arrangement cannot survive.

The system does not want upward mobility for those without parental support or network access. It wants compliance. And when compliance is not forthcoming, it wants exclusion.

---

## A.9 The Penalty's Deniability

The penalty must be deniable, and it is therefore performed through techniques that can each be disavowed:

- "I didn't understand you." — The problem is your clarity, not my recognition.
- "We just thought someone else was a better fit." — The problem is fit, not credential.
- "I forgot to include you." — The problem is my memory, not my intention.
- "I do not condone the patriarchy." — The problem is your ideology, not your physiology.
- "There's no need to be aggressive." — The problem is your tone, not my refusal to hear it.
- "That wasn't your job." — The problem is your overreach, not the assignee's neglect, not the harm to stakeholders, and not my refusal to credit the work you did after waiting and offering the credit to the person responsible.
- "You have VWO but you never finished university." — The problem is your status, not your performance.
- "You make others uncomfortable." — The problem is your excellence, not my inability to acknowledge it.

None of these statements names the real cause, because naming it would require admitting that the coercion authority exists, that it grounds the speaker's own position, and that presence, performance, and remediation can all threaten it. That admission is unavailable. So the penalty proceeds through displacement — real in its effect, deniable in its expression.

Bourdieu's (1991) concept of *symbolic violence* describes this precisely: violence that cannot be named without the sufferer appearing paranoid, aggressive, or entitled. Goffman's (1963) analysis of *civil inattention* — the ritual by which strangers in public acknowledge each other's presence without engaging — is relevant here, but inverted. In Goffman's account, civil inattention is a form of respect. In the account, the withholding of recognition functions as its opposite: the interlocutor performs the form of civil inattention while using it to deny the subject's presence or his contribution altogether.

---

## A.10 Why Both Men and Women Enforce It

The penalty is enforced across genders, and the methods differ according to the position each holds within the arrangement. This is not a claim about women, or about Dutch women, or about gender generally. It is a claim about the internal logic of the arrangement.

**Those who have paid the credential price** — typically men who have endured initiation, joined the association, and learned to administer what they once received — hold a credential. The subject holds comparable authority without the credential. Every interaction in which the subject is heard, or credited, without having paid devalues what the other person bought. Quiet exclusion — withholding recognition, omitting from collaboration, taking credit, rebuking overreach — is credential defence. Its purpose is not to harm the subject but to restore the fiction that authority must be purchased.

**Those whose participation and silence are required by the arrangement** — a position often occupied by women, but not exclusively — may defend it by reframing unmediated presence as an ideology. The denunciation — "I do not condone the patriarchy" — converts the subject's presence into a political statement he did not make. If his presence is an ideology, then compliance with the arrangement becomes resistance to him rather than submission to it.

The arrangement is not a simple patriarchy. It is a coercion hierarchy that affects everyone, though differently. Women are themselves subject to coercion authority: the main paper documents how they must pay a price to gain access to the credential. The defences they deploy are not expressions of gendered malice; they are strategies of survival within a structure that punishes those who do not defend it.

## A.11 The Recruitment Apparatus: Moral Inversion and the Predator Crew

### A.11.1 The Moral Inversion

The most revealing element of the account is the framing used in the recruitment: exploitation presented as charity.

This inversion is documented in the Dutch context. NPO Radio 1 reports that "Elk jaar lopen honderdduizenden studenten stage met als doel om relevante werkervaring op te doen, maar in werkelijkheid worden stagiairs regelmatig vooral als goedkope werkkracht gebruikt" (Every year hundreds of thousands of students do internships with the goal of gaining relevant work experience, but in reality interns are regularly used primarily as cheap labour). FNV Young & United reports that "Jongeren kunnen zich nergens op beroepen. Je kan je niet beroepen op het arbeidsrecht en daardoor maken veel werkgevers gebruik van stagiaires" (Young people cannot appeal to anything. You cannot appeal to labour law and therefore many employers use interns). A Sagepub article on "precarious commitment" shows that "the unspoken and hence hidden character of gift-giving opens opportunities for exploitation without accountability".

The Dutch case, as the main paper argued, gives this technique a theological character. Where the moral hierarchy turns on a distinction between those who passed the test and those who failed it, exploitation of the failed is not merely permissible — it is justified. They failed. They are deficient. To give them work, even exploitative work, is to give them what they deserve: a place in the order that has already judged them. Exploitation becomes grace.

**The moral inversion is the arrangement's immune response to ethical objection.** An economic system that exploits can be criticised and reformed. A moral order that reframes exploitation as charity cannot be criticised without attacking the order itself. The critic is not a critic of business practice. The critic is a heretic. This is why the subject describes the system as religious and spiritual rather than merely economic.

### A.11.2 The Predator Crew as Conversion Mechanism

The account uses the term "predator crew" for the informal layer of supervisors, team leads, and small operators who administer extraction at ground level. The term names a class the main paper described only abstractly: neither elite nor prey, but operators.

This layer does not own the system. Its members are frequently precarious themselves. What they hold is a licence: the right to extract from those below them in exchange for loyalty to those above. The subject was offered a place in this layer, not as a reward for virtue but as recognition of strength.

The predator crew is grounded in the literature on "perpetrator predation." A Cambridge Core article proposes this framework: "a 'predator' is a person who 'lives by predation,' or 'looks for other people in order to use, control, or harm them in some way'". The framework "draws our attention to the individual who targets those characteristics" and "brings power into the picture, with power disparities often separating predator from prey". It notes that "potential aggressors choose targets strategically, focusing their hostility on people who seem difficult to like and/or those who appear to be vulnerable and unable to defend themselves".

The predator crew is also a variant of what Lipsky (1980) called street-level bureaucrats: frontline workers who exercise substantial discretion in implementing policy, and who use that discretion to manage their own working conditions. It is an instance of what Arendt (1963) and Browning (1992) documented: middle-layer operators who administer extraction without ideological commitment.

**The predator crew is the mechanism by which potential threats are converted into assets, and by which unauthorised authority is converted into authorised authority.** The subject was offered a place in this layer. Acceptance would have converted him from a threat into an asset. Refusal left him as a threat — and a threat that could not be absorbed was a threat that had to be excluded.

## A.12 The Statistics as Instrument of Exclusion

The main paper documented how the "average" rent, the "independent student" category, and the "success" of the system are all skewed by the inclusion of populations that are not representative. This appendix extends the argument: the statistics are not merely skewed; they are an instrument of exclusion.

The "average rent" is not a market price. It is a network price, subsidised by the segment of the market accessible only through compliance. The student who pays €900 is not paying the average; they are paying the residual market price — the price that remains after the network has taken its share. The average is pulled down by the rooms allocated through compliance-gated networks. Those rooms are not available to students who decline the compliance price. The student who declines is not an average case; they are a forced high-price case.

The "independent student" category is not a measure of independence. It is a composite of international students (who are *uitwonend* by necessity), wealthy independents (whose parents have purchased property), and fraternity members (who have access to the network rent). The genuinely independent Dutch student without parental support or network access is a shrinking minority within a category that is itself shrinking.

The "success" of the system is measured by outputs that exclude the filtered-out. The students who gave up searching for housing are not counted in the shortage. The students who left the system are not counted in the retention statistics. The students who were excluded from the network are not counted in the average. A full accounting of the system's output must include those who were filtered out.

**The statistics produce an image of a functioning system while concealing the mechanism that excludes those without credentials.** They are not neutral. They are an instrument of the arrangement.

## A.13 Why This Is Doctrine

The main paper argued that the Dutch system is not accidental — it is enforced. This appendix argues something further: the mechanism is not merely enforced but *believed*.

No policy document produces the sequence described here. No statute requires the embodied penalty, the demoralisation, the recruitment, or the inversion. What produces them is a social practice transmitted through institutions, families, workplaces, and ordinary interactions, and legitimised by a moral inheritance that distinguishes the tested from the failed and the tested from the untested.

This is what makes "doctrine" the right word. A policy can be changed by legislation. A doctrine is reproduced by belief. Within the account, the exploitation of the vulnerable is not presented as a regrettable necessity but as a good — an opportunity, a gift. The system does not apologise for extraction. It presents extraction as the natural order.

The doctrine is the reason the mechanism is so resistant to reform. It is not enough to change the rules; the belief that the rules are legitimate must be challenged. This is why the appendix's critique is foundational: it challenges not just the policy but the doctrine. It requires a transformation of belief.

---

## A.14 Implications for Foundational Change

The analysis implies six changes.

**First, the credential must be decoupled from coercion.** Authority should not be granted only to those who have paid for it through hazing, exploitation, or silence. Alternative paths to authority must be created and legitimised. This means reforming fraternity and association cultures, but it also means reforming workplaces and institutions that reward credential defence over competence.

**Second, unmediated authority must be recognised as legitimate.** A deep voice, a broad frame, a confident presence — these are not attacks. They are forms of human diversity. Institutions must develop norms that allow for the expression of presence without treating it as an ideology or a threat. This requires explicit discussion of tone policing, implicit bias, and the difference between assertiveness and aggression.

**Third, uncredentialed merit must be rewarded.** Value produced outside the assigned role should be recognised and credited. The practice of extracting labour and then disavowing it must be named and stopped. Performance evaluation should focus on what is produced, not on who is authorised to produce it.

**Fourth, the moral inversion must be reversed.** Exploitation is not charity. Extraction is not opportunity. The reframing of exploitation as a gift must be exposed and rejected. This requires a moral vocabulary that names exploitation as exploitation, without the theological gloss that justifies it.

**Fifth, the predator crew must be dismantled.** The informal layer of operators who administer extraction at ground level must be replaced by structures that do not require the exploitation of the vulnerable. This means reforming labour markets, internship systems, and welfare-to-work programmes so that they do not depend on the availability of people who can be paid less than a living wage.

**Sixth, the statistics must be corrected.** A full accounting of the system's output must include those who were filtered out. The average rent, the independent student category, and the success of the system must be recalculated to include the students who gave up, the students who were excluded, and the students who left.

These are not modest reforms. They challenge the foundational logic of the arrangement. And because the arrangement is a doctrine — because it is believed — reform requires more than policy change. It requires a transformation of belief.

## A.15 Anticipated Objections

### A.15.1 The account is a single case.

The appendix's method is the case study, which is a legitimate method for illuminating mechanisms, generating concepts, and tracing sequences. The account's value is not that it is representative but that it is diagnostic. Its claims are testable against additional cases. §A.15.7 specifies what would count against the readings.

### A.15.2 The interpretations are not verified independently.

Interpretations cannot be verified independently, but they can be assessed for consistency with the main paper's documented structural findings, with external sources, and for internal coherence. The interpretations offered here meet all three criteria. They also prefer the more conservative reading where interpretations compete.

### A.15.3 The mechanism explains everything.

The mechanism explains a specific class of responses — penalties for unauthorised authority — and predicts specific forms these penalties will take. It does not predict which form will occur in any given case. The typology is falsifiable: if penalties for unauthorised authority occurred in contexts where credentials were not valued, the mechanism would be weakened.

### A.15.4 The scream could be read differently.

The scream could be read as an expression of feminist discourse with its own complex relationship to Dutch institutional life. The coercion-authority reading is preferred because the scream occurred before the subject had spoken, which means it cannot be a response to anything he said. The alternative reading is not excluded; it is simply weaker.

### A.15.5 The predator crew is empirically thin.

The predator crew is a concept, not an empirical finding. It names a layer that the main paper documents structurally: middle management of exploitative housing, operators of the fraternity gatekeeper, administrators of welfare fraud penalties. Its grounding in the perpetrator-predation literature and in street-level bureaucracy strengthens the concept's theoretical basis.

### A.15.6 The Dutch specificity is asserted more than demonstrated.

The appendix does not claim that the mechanisms are uniquely Dutch. It claims that their specific forms in the account are consistent with the Dutch structural patterns the main paper documents. The concepts are portable; the local forms are contingent. The external sources cited — voice-perception research, Reddit accounts, tone-policing literature, Rutgers ostracism research, the Tall Poppy Trap, perpetrator predation, and Dutch internship-exploitation journalism — establish that the mechanisms are general while the Dutch expression is one case among many.

### A.15.7 What Would Count Against This Reading

The reading offered in this appendix would be weakened by any of the following:

- If the subject were rewarded for unauthorised performance, the credential-defence reading would be weakened.
- If the exclusion were demonstrably driven by explicit ideology, personal conflict, or incompetence — and not by credential defence — the reading would be weakened.
- If the parties who enforced the penalty had no credential themselves, the reading would be complicated.
- If the same dynamics occurred with equal intensity in contexts where credentials were not valued, the reading would be weakened.
- If the subject's account were shown to systematically misdescribe events, the reading would be significantly weakened.

The appendix cannot test these falsifiers because it has only one account. But by specifying them, it moves from ideology (unfalsifiable) to theory (falsifiable in principle).

### A.15.8 Testable Implications

The mechanism predicts that penalties for unauthorised authority will take specific forms (exclusion, demotion, rebuke, freeze-out, absorption, moral inversion), that they will be deniable, and that they will be administered by those with a stake in the credential system. These predictions can be tested against additional cases, including cases in other institutional contexts and other national settings.

---

## A.16 Conclusion

The appendix has argued that the Dutch system operates a coercion authority that is granted only to those who have paid for it through participation in and administration of coercion. The account documents three demonstrations that the credential is not necessary: presence shows that authority can exist without it; performance shows that value can be produced without it; remediation shows that duty can be discharged without it.

Each demonstration was met with penalties that were deniable, disproportionate, and administered by those with a stake in the credential system. The penalties reveal the credential as a fiction maintained by exclusion.

The system does not want upward mobility for those without parental support or network access. It wants compliance. And when compliance is not forthcoming, it wants exclusion.

The subject was excluded not because he was threatening. He was excluded because he was evidence.
