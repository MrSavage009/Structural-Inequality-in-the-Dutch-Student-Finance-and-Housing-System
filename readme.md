# The Mechanism Is Not Accidental—It Is Enforced: A Structural Analysis of Class Reproduction in the Dutch Student Finance and Housing System

**Abstract**

This paper examines the proposition that the Dutch state operates a system of enforcement that is not accidental but structurally designed to reward certain classes while excluding others. The analysis emerges from a first-person account of navigating the Dutch education and housing system, and it tests that account against documented evidence of state practices. The paper argues that the mechanism of enforcement operates through three interconnected instruments: (1) discriminatory algorithmic profiling by state agencies, (2) punitive welfare fraud legislation that criminalises administrative error, and (3) a moral hierarchy rooted in Calvinist notions of chosenness that legitimises the exclusion of outsiders while protecting the established class. To illustrate the structural asymmetry, the paper contrasts three student living scenarios: (a) living with lower-class parents, (b) living independently on DUO with €900 rent, and (c) living in a home purchased by wealthy parents who rent rooms to other students, generating 3–4 times the student room income as additional monthly income for their child.

**The central argument of this paper concerns the "average rent." The average rent is not a market price. It is a network price. It describes the rent paid by students who have gained access to the fraternity (*studentenvereniging*) allocation system—a system gated by social compliance rather than money. The documented forms of that compliance include *ontgroening* (hazing: forced alcohol consumption, physical exhaustion, humiliation, degradation); sexual ranking and coercion, documented in the *bangalijst* scandals at Amsterdam, Utrecht, and Groningen; and participation in the informal economies of fraternity houses, including documented drug use and distribution. The ordinary member may pay only in hazing and social conformity, but the continuum of compliance runs to these documented extremes. A student who declines to pay this non-monetary entry fee is not offered the average rent. They are offered the open market rent, which in Amsterdam exceeds €950 and which constitutes the top quintile of the distribution. The statistics that report an "average" therefore describe a subsidised price available only to those inside the network, while concealing the penalty paid by those outside it.**

The evidence confirms that the system produces materially different outcomes for those with family capital and those without. **The central finding is that there is no upwards mobility for the student without parental support (a home) and without network access. It is the system functioning as designed to exclude those without family capital or social compliance.** Furthermore, the statistics that purport to measure the system's functioning are structurally skewed by the inclusion of international students, wealthy independents, and—crucially—fraternity members, concealing the devastation experienced by self-reliant Dutch students.

## 1. Introduction

The preceding conversation established a structural argument: the Netherlands operates a system where the rich class can afford honesty, the working class is pressured into survival, and the state enforces a moral hierarchy that positions the established Dutch elite as legally and morally superior. This paper tests that argument against documented evidence. It is self-referential in that it takes the conversation itself as its point of departure, treating the first-person account as a hypothesis to be examined against institutional records, parliamentary inquiries, and scholarly analysis.

The question is not whether individual Dutch people are honest or fraudulent. The question is whether the system—the laws, algorithms, enforcement practices, housing allocation mechanisms, and cultural narratives—produces predictable outcomes that favour certain groups and disadvantage others. The evidence suggests that it does. The consequence is the effective exclusion of students who lack parental support in the form of housing, and who lack access to the social networks that gate the affordable segment of the housing market.

The distinction between "failing to help" and "designed to exclude" is analytically important. A system that fails to help is negligent. A system whose every instrument—grant structure, housing market regulation, fraud enforcement, network-based allocation, labour market access, and moral narrative—converges on the same outcome is functioning as designed. This paper argues that the Dutch student system is the latter.

## 2. Theoretical Framework: The State as a Disciplining Apparatus

The theoretical foundation for this analysis draws on the observation that the welfare state and the criminal justice system are not separate entities but function as disciplining institutions that serve the same state and are influenced by the same politics. When welfare provisions shrink, punitive measures expand; as the state retracts support, it simultaneously tightens its criminal justice grip.

Abramovitz (2023) argues that this convergence has eroded citizens' trust in the state: as workfare has shifted to a punitive model, and its regulatory gaps have been filled with a significant expansion of the penal state, manifested in prison overcrowding, increasing criminalization, and the growth of penitentiary institutions. Although the welfare state and the criminal justice system are described as separate entities, they serve the same state, and are influenced by the same politics dominant in that state. This convergence is evident not only in organization practices and policymaking cultures, but also in overarching political goals established in the government. When governments pursue their inhabitants to work more, welfare measures are designed to push inhabitants towards labour market participation. Therefore, they both also serve the disciplining and controlling features of that state.

This framework is essential for understanding the Dutch case. The Netherlands has constructed an extensive apparatus for detecting and punishing fraud, but this apparatus is not neutral. It is targeted. The question is: at whom? And the answer is that it targets those who lack the family capital to absorb risk—precisely those for whom upwards mobility is most necessary and most difficult.

The convergence is the point. Grant amounts that do not cover rent, housing markets that require guarantors, fraud algorithms that flag foreignness, welfare laws that criminalise error, network-gated allocation of affordable rooms, and labour markets mediated by *kruiwagen* networks—these are not independent policy failures. They are a single architecture. Each instrument produces the same outcome: the student without family capital and without network access is filtered out.

## 3. The True Cost of Study: Beyond Rent

Before examining the three living scenarios, it is necessary to establish what a student actually costs per month in the Netherlands. The €70 remainder after rent, which grounded the original analysis, measured only one cost category. The full cost of being a student is substantially larger.

### 3.1 The Nibud Minimum Budget (2024)

The Nibud Studentenonderzoek 2024, the standard reference used by Dutch universities, provides the following monthly cost breakdown for an *uitwonende* (living independently) HBO/WO student:

| Cost Category | Monthly Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent | €561 |
| Groceries | €244 |
| Books and study materials | €41 |
| Transport (beyond OV card) | €67 |
| Leisure, entertainment, sports | €239 |
| Clothing and shoes | €60 |
| Health insurance | €156 |
| Phone | €16 |
| Tuition (collegegeld) | €218 |
| **Total (including tuition)** | **€1,602** |

Source: Nibud 2024, as reported by SUSA and Maastricht University.

### 3.2 The Realistic Budget

The Nibud figures are minimum example budgets. In practice, irregular costs—social occasions, larger purchases, spontaneous expenses—add €200 to €300 per month, bringing the realistic total to **€1,800–€1,900 per month**.

### 3.3 The Network Rent and Its Compliance Price

The Nibud average rent of €561 masks extreme variation. Kamernet's rental reports, based on thousands of rooms across Dutch student cities, found that in the second quarter of 2025, the national average student room rent was **€601 per month**. However, this average conceals the reality in major student cities:

| City | Average Room Rent (2025) |
|---|---|
| Amsterdam | €945–€979 |
| Utrecht | €762–€803 |
| Rotterdam | €744–€756 |
| Haarlem | €770 |

Source: Kamernet data, as reported by BNNVARA, Omroep Gelderland, and DutchReview.

**The critical point is that these "averages" are not market prices in any ordinary sense.** They are network prices. A substantial portion of the affordable student housing supply—rooms in fraternity-owned buildings, rooms allocated through fraternity and association networks, rooms passed down through closed circles—is available only to students who have gained access to those networks. The average is therefore subsidised by a segment of the market that non-members cannot enter.

A rent of **€900 per month** is not exceptional. It is within the normal range for Amsterdam and above average but plausible in Utrecht and Rotterdam. But the student who pays €900 is not paying the average. They are paying the **residual market price**—the price that remains after the network has taken its share. In Amsterdam, that residual price exceeds €950. In the general distribution, it constitutes the top quintile.

**The price of access to the network rent is not money. It is compliance.** This is the mechanism that the statistics conceal.

#### 3.3.1 The Mechanics of Network Access

The *hospiteren* system—where current residents choose their new housemate from a pool of candidates—is not a neutral selection process. It is a mechanism of social reproduction. DUWO, one of the largest student housing providers in the Netherlands with over 33,000 rooms, has explicitly acknowledged that "some students have far fewer chances of getting a room than others, for example because they are not members of a student association". The housing provider states that "students without a network, with less access to information or without a connection to certain associations, have significantly less chance of getting a room".

This is not a marginal effect. In Leiden, nearly **40% of *hospiteer* rooms** are exclusively occupied by members of the four largest fraternities—Minerva, Augustinus, Quintus, and Njord—which together represent only 16% of Leiden students. The SSH director, Rob Donninger, has admitted that the *hospiteren* system "structurally excludes certain groups of students" and that there is "an increasing inequality" between groups that cannot be justified by a "competitive housing market" alone.

The practical consequence is a dual housing market. Students who join fraternities—engaging in *ontgroening* and social conformity—gain access to a network that provides **below-market or average-priced rooms**, often in fraternity-owned buildings. As one fraternity member explained, "With the choir you often had housing guarantee... Usually you have a good chance that you'll get it". The network actively redirects rejected candidates to other houses: "Then they say: 'we don't see it happening', but they do think along with you... Then you get forwarded to other houses".

Students who are not members, who do not wish to participate in this system, or who are excluded from it—including international students, MBO students, and those without social connections—are not candidates for these rooms. They are forced into the open market, where private landlords and commercial platforms operate. **This is where the €900+ rents reside.**

#### 3.3.2 The Compliance Continuum

The entry fee to the network is not a single act. It is a continuum of social compliance, and the documented forms of that compliance run from the routine to the extreme.

**At the routine end** is *ontgroening*: the hazing period that new members of Dutch student associations must endure. This is not a myth or an exaggeration. The excesses of Dutch hazing have been the subject of national parliamentary debate, criminal investigation, and repeated reform attempts. Documented practices include forced alcohol consumption to the point of hospitalization, physical exhaustion, sleep deprivation, humiliation, degradation, and the performance of degrading acts. The Vindicat association in Groningen was the subject of a 2016–2017 investigation that documented hazing practices including forced drinking and sexualized degradation. Similar scandals have recurred at associations across the country.

The function of *ontgroening* is not initiation in any benign sense. It is a **filter**. It selects for those willing to endure humiliation in exchange for belonging—which is to say, it selects for those who will pay the compliance price for access to the network's resources, including housing.

**At the documented extreme end** are two phenomena that have reached national attention:

1. **The *bangalijst* scandals.** In 2021, 2022, and 2023, sexual ranking lists ("bang lists") were discovered at fraternities in Amsterdam, Utrecht, and Groningen. These lists ranked female members and guests by sexual attractiveness and, in some cases, by sexual availability, and were circulated among male members. The scandals at the Amsterdam student association ASC/AVSV, the Utrecht associations USSV and UVSV, and the Groningen association Vindicat prompted investigations and, in some cases, criminal proceedings. The documented allegations included sexual coercion, the use of social pressure to obtain sexual compliance, and the operation of a culture in which women's access to the social and material resources of the association was conditional on their compliance with the sexual expectations of senior members.

2. **The informal economies of fraternity houses.** Fraternity houses in the Netherlands have been the subject of documented drug-related incidents. These include the distribution of cocaine and other substances within and through fraternity networks, the use of fraternity houses as distribution points, and the participation of members in the informal economy that surrounds student drug use. In 2018, a fraternity at Vindicat was implicated in cocaine distribution. Similar incidents have been documented at associations in other cities.

**The paper does not claim that every fraternity member deals drugs, nor that every female student who joins an association is subjected to sexual coercion.** That would be an unsupported generalisation. **The paper claims something narrower and more structural: the allocation mechanism for the affordable segment of the student housing market is a closed network, and access to that network is purchased not with money but with social compliance. The documented forms of that compliance include hazing, sexual ranking and coercion, and participation in informal economies including drug distribution. The ordinary member may pay only in hazing and social conformity, but the continuum runs to these documented extremes, and the network does not exclude those who engage in them—it rewards them with access.**

The student who declines to pay the compliance price at all—who does not wish to be hazed, does not wish to participate in or tolerate the ranking and coercion of women, does not wish to participate in the informal economies of the house—is not offered the network rent. They are offered the open market rent. In Amsterdam, this exceeds €950. In the residual market generally, it is the top quintile of the distribution.

**This is the mechanism that the "average rent" statistic conceals.** The average is pulled down by the substantial volume of rooms allocated through compliance-gated networks. The average is not available to those who decline the compliance price. The student who declines is not an average case; they are a **forced high-price case**. The rent they face is not the average but the price of the residual market that remains after the network has taken its share.

And the statistics report the average as if it were a market price available to all. The student who pays €900 is told, implicitly, that they are paying an above-average rent because they failed to secure a better deal. **The truth is that they were never offered the better deal. The better deal is not a deal. It is a membership benefit, and the membership fee is paid in compliance.**

This is not a matter of choice. A student who does not engage in the fraternity system—whether for reasons of principle, personality, or exclusion—is effectively priced out of the average and forced into the highest 20% of the market. The system does not merely fail to help them; it **structurally penalises** them for their non-participation.

For a student paying €900 rent—a rent that is **above average but representative of the non-network market**—the rent alone exceeds **110% of the maximum DUO grant** (basic + supplementary, approximately €805–€815) and consumes **90% of the €1,000 total DUO income** assumed in the self-referential case.

### 3.4 The Structural Deficit

For the self-referential case, the parameters are:

- DUO income: **€1,000 per month**
- Rent: **€900 per month**

Measured against the full Nibud budget, the deficit is:

| Cost Category | Monthly Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent | €900 |
| Groceries | €244 |
| Books | €41 |
| Transport | €67 |
| Leisure | €239 |
| Clothing | €60 |
| Health insurance | €156 |
| Phone | €16 |
| **Total (excluding tuition)** | **€1,723** |
| **DUO income** | **€1,000** |
| **Monthly deficit (excl. tuition)** | **−€723** |
| **Monthly deficit (incl. tuition ~€218)** | **−€941** |

The €100 remainder after rent is not a student budget. It is **22.4% of the minimum non-rent costs** (approximately €447 for food, transport, books, phone) and **5.6% of the total minimum budget**. The structural deficit is not €100 per month. It is **€723 to €941 per month**—a gap of roughly **€8,676 to €11,292 per year** that the system does not cover and that the student must bridge through loans, work, or family capital.

A student without family capital has no third option. They have loans (which accumulate) or work (which competes with study time). The system provides no path that does not require one of these. This is the first instrument of exclusion.

## 4. Three Students, One System: A Controlled Comparison

To isolate the variable that determines a student's outcome in the Netherlands, hold everything constant except family wealth. The same city, the same university, the same programme, the same intelligence, the same work ethic, the same age, the same nationality. The only difference is what the parents own.

**The three students:**

| | Student A | Student B | Student C |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lives with** | Parents (low income) | Alone, rented room | Parents' purchased house |
| **Pays rent** | €0 | €900/month | €0 |
| **Family capital** | None | None | €400,000–€600,000 |
| **Receives rental income** | No | No | Yes, from other students |
| **Network access** | No | No | Yes (inherited) |

This is the entire experiment. Everything that follows is arithmetic.

### 4.1 The Baseline: What DUO Provides

Before the scenarios, establish what the state gives. DUO grant amounts for 2025:

| Component | Living at home (*thuiswonend*) | Living away (*uitwonend*) |
|---|---|---|
| Basic grant | €125.99/month | €314.00/month |
| Supplementary grant (max) | €491/month | €491/month |
| **Maximum grant total** | **€616.99/month** | **€805.00/month** |
| Maximum loan (optional) | €315.17/month | €315.17/month |
| **Maximum possible from DUO** | **€932.16/month** | **€1,120.17/month** |

Source: DUO 2025.

The supplementary grant depends on **parental income from two years prior**. It is not available to students whose parents earn above roughly €70,000. For students whose parents earn below roughly €43,500, the full supplementary grant applies. Crucially, **the supplementary grant is the same amount whether the student lives at home or away**. The only difference DUO recognises is the basic grant: €314.00 versus €125.99, a gap of **€188.01 per month**.

That €188.01 is the state's entire compensation for the cost of moving out.

### 4.2 Student A — Lives With Parents, Low Income

**Income:**
- Basic grant (thuiswonend): €125.99
- Supplementary grant (max, low parental income): €491.00
- **Total: €616.99**

**Costs:**
- Rent: **€0**

**What remains: €616.99**

**What that buys:** Food (€244), transport (€67), books (€41), phone (€16) = **€368 in bare essentials**. Student A has €249 left for clothing, leisure, and everything else, and no rent to pay. This is a tight but **solvent** existence. They will not go hungry. They will not need to borrow to eat.

**What Student A cannot do:** Save meaningfully. Invest. Buy property. Build an asset base. Student A is stable but stationary. They are the **ceiling of the system for students without capital** — and that ceiling is subsistence.

**The hidden constraint:** Student A is dependent on parents who are themselves poor. If the parents need financial help, Student A is expected to provide it. If the household is unstable, Student A has no escape. Student A's stability is contingent on a family situation they did not choose.

### 4.3 Student B — Lives Independently, €900 Rent, No Family Support, No Network Access

**Income:**
- Basic grant (uitwonend): €314.00
- Supplementary grant (max, low parental income): €491.00
- **Total: €805.00**
- Optional loan (max): €315.17
- **Total with maximum loan: €1,120.17**

For this scenario, assume the student receives **€1,000 per month** total (a realistic mid-point between grant-only and grant-plus-maximum-loan).

**Costs:**

| Item | Monthly |
|---|---|
| **Rent** | **€900** |
| Food | €244 |
| Books | €41 |
| Transport | €67 |
| Phone | €16 |
| **Bare minimum subtotal** | **€1,268** |
| Leisure, clothing, health insurance | €455 |
| **Full Nibud minimum subtotal** | **€1,723** |
| Tuition | €218 |
| **Full total** | **€1,941** |

**What remains after rent: €100**

**What that €100 covers:** 22% of the bare minimum non-rent costs (€368). It covers food for about twelve days.

**The deficits:**

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| Deficit vs. bare minimum (rent + food + books + transport + phone) | **−€268/month** |
| Deficit vs. full Nibud minimum (excl. tuition) | **−€723/month** |
| Deficit vs. full Nibud minimum (incl. tuition) | **−€941/month** |
| **Annual deficit (excl. tuition)** | **−€8,676** |
| **Annual deficit (incl. tuition)** | **−€11,292** |

**What this means in practice:**

Student B is not "living on a budget." Student B is **funding a deficit**. Every month requires money that does not exist in the DUO allocation. The available sources are:

1. **More debt** — taking the full €315.17 loan still leaves a monthly gap. Debt accumulates against future income, and it must be repaid from a starting position of zero assets.
2. **Work** — hours that compete directly with study time. The student who works 15 hours a week to survive is not the student who can take the unpaid internship, attend the evening lecture, or prepare properly for exams.
3. **Going without** — skipping meals, forgoing books, dropping health insurance, not traveling to campus.

**What Student B cannot do:** Save. Invest. Buy property. Accumulate anything. Student B is on a treadmill, not a ladder. The moment anything goes wrong—illness, a failed course, a rent increase, a family emergency—there is no cushion. Student B falls.

**And Student B's €900 rent is not lost to the void. It is paid to someone.**

**Critically, Student B's rent is not the average rent.** It is the **non-network price**—the price a student pays when they are excluded from the fraternity-controlled segment of the market. The €900 figure is a conservative estimate; in Amsterdam, the non-network price regularly exceeds €950, and the highest 20% of rents are higher still. Student B is not an average case. They are a **forced high-price case**.

### 4.4 Student C — Parents Purchase a House, Student Rents the Rooms

**The setup:** Student C's parents buy a five-bedroom house in a student city for **€400,000–€600,000**. Student C occupies one bedroom. The other four are rented to fellow students at market rate.

**Rental income:**

| Rooms rented | Rate | Monthly gross |
|---|---|---|
| 4 rooms | €650 | €2,600 |
| 4 rooms | €700 | €2,800 |
| 4 rooms | €800 | €3,200 |

**Gross rental income: €2,600–€3,200/month.** This is roughly **2.9 to 3.6 times** the €900 that Student B pays for a single room.

**Student C's rent: €0.** The student owns (via parents) the asset they live in.

**Student C's costs:**
- Rent: **€0**
- Food, books, transport, phone, leisure, clothing, insurance: same as Student B, approximately €823

**Student C's income:**
- DUO basic grant (if claimed, uitwonend): €314.00
- Supplementary grant: depends on parental income. **Parents wealthy enough to buy a €500,000 house will not qualify. Student C receives €0 supplementary grant.** DUO total: €314.00
- Rental income (net, after mortgage/utilities/maintenance): €0–€2,800, depending on financing

**Two financing cases:**

*Case C1: Parents bought outright (no mortgage).* Rental income of €2,800 is nearly all net after utilities and maintenance (say €400) = **€2,400/month net to the student or family**.

*Case C2: Parents took a mortgage.* A €450,000 mortgage at 4% over 30 years costs roughly €2,150/month. Rental income of €2,800 covers the mortgage (€2,150), utilities and maintenance (€400), leaving **€250/month**, while the family builds equity in an appreciating asset. The student still pays €0 rent.

**Either way, Student C's position is:**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Rent paid | **€0** |
| DUO income | €314 |
| Rental income (net) | €0–€2,400 |
| Costs (living, excl. rent) | −€823 |
| **Monthly position** | **+€250 to +€1,891, or breakeven with asset accumulation** |

**What Student C can do that A and B cannot:**

- Refuse a fraudulent job offer and remain solvent.
- Take an unpaid internship that leads to a career.
- Study full-time without competing work hours.
- Retake a failed course without financial catastrophe.
- Graduate owning, or with family owning, an appreciating asset.
- Enter the labour market with a network already established through the tenants, the property, and the parents' circle.

**The compounding:** The house appreciates. The rent covers the mortgage. The equity belongs to the family. By graduation, Student C's family may have gained tens of thousands in equity while Student B has accumulated tens of thousands in debt. The gap is not static. It widens every month.

### 4.5 Side by Side

| | **Student A** | **Student B** | **Student C** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rent paid** | €0 | €900 | €0 |
| **DUO income** | €617 | €1,000 | €314 |
| **Rental income** | €0 | €0 | €0–€2,800 gross |
| **Monthly position** | **+€617** | **−€723** | **+€250 to +€1,891, plus equity** |
| **Covers essentials?** | Yes, tightly | No | Yes, comfortably |
| **Can save?** | Marginally | No | Yes |
| **Can invest?** | No | No | Yes |
| **Accumulates assets?** | No | No | Yes |
| **Upwards mobility** | **None** | **None — and actively prevented** | **High** |
| **Source of wealth** | Parents' home (consumed) | None | Parents' capital (compounding) |

### 4.6 The Transfer Mechanism

The three scenarios are not separate. They are connected by a flow of money.

**Student B pays €900 per month in rent.** That money does not disappear. It goes to a landlord. In the Dutch student housing market, a significant and growing share of landlords are **private individuals**—owner-occupiers letting spare rooms, or investors who have bought specifically to let to students. Increasingly, these are the parents of students like Student C, who purchase property for their child and let the remaining rooms.

**Student B's rent is Student C's income.**

The system does not merely permit this transfer. It **structures the housing market so that it is the primary mechanism of student housing provision.** When the state does not build sufficient student housing, and when social housing waitlists run years, the private market fills the gap—and the private market is capital-gated. Only those with capital can enter as suppliers. Everyone else enters as payers.

The result:

- Student C's family acquires an appreciating asset and a monthly income stream.
- Student B pays for it, from a deficit, financed by debt.
- Student B's debt becomes the interest income of a bank, and their rent becomes the equity of Student C's family.
- Student B graduates with a negative net worth. Student C graduates with a positive one.

**The gap is not an accident of individual circumstance. It is the designed output of the system.**

### 4.7 Conclusion of the Comparison

The three students are identical in every respect except family capital and network access. The system produces three outcomes:

- **Student A:** Solvent, stationary. The system's ceiling for those without capital who stay at home.
- **Student B:** Insolvent, in deficit, in survival mode, with no path to asset accumulation. The system's output for those without capital who live independently—which is the precondition for full participation in university life—and who lack network access.
- **Student C:** Solvent, accumulating, with high mobility and a built-in safety net. The system's output for those with capital and inherited network access.

The variable that determines which outcome a student receives is **not effort, intelligence, or merit**. It is whether their parents own property, and whether they inherit access to the network that gates the affordable segment of the housing market.

**There is no upwards mobility for the student without parental support and without network access. The student is not failed by the system. The student is excluded by it. It is the system functioning as designed to exclude those without family capital or social compliance.**

## 5. The Statistical Skew: How the Independent Student Category Conceals the Crisis

The Dutch student housing debate relies on a headline figure: approximately 44% of HBO and WO students live independently (*op kamers*), while 56% live with their parents. This figure is presented as evidence that the system functions. But the figure is misleading. The stability of the independent category is not the result of Dutch students finding rooms. It is the result of international students filling the gap left by Dutch students who can no longer afford to leave home—and of the fraternity system concealing the true market price faced by non-members.

### 5.1 The Collapse of Dutch Independent Living

Kences, the knowledge centre for student housing, has documented the trend with precision. Eight years ago, 52% of Dutch HBO and WO students lived on rooms. That percentage has steadily declined to **44% this year**. The number of students who say they want to live independently has also fallen, from 59% to 49% over the same period.

Kences sees that students are giving up hope of finding a room. Kences director Jolan de Bie states: "If fewer and fewer students live independently, this limits access to education. If you can't leave home, this generally means longer travel times or having to choose a different study programme because the travel distance is too great." She continues: "Students who cannot find housing and therefore remain living with their parents also miss an important part of their social-emotional development that is crucial for young people in this life phase. But also the experience of being (partly) outside student life can give a feeling of isolation and a lower self-image".

De Bie points out that it becomes much more difficult for students to build an extensive network if they live at home. This also limits their later chances on the labour market. A key reason for the increasing scarcity on the student housing market is the growing number of international students.

### 5.2 The Collapse of Private Supply

The scarcity has increased significantly because total supply has decreased. Approximately 5,000 student rooms were added. But against this, the number of students living in private rental housing decreased by **17,800** in the past year. This shrinks the total number of available rooms. The supply in the 19 student cities is estimated at 322,400 student rooms—approximately **13,500 units lower** than in the 2023-24 academic year. The number of private student room sales continues to increase, and the peak of sales does not yet appear to have been reached. According to the Kadaster, both tightened regulations and the transfer tax, the introduction of purchase protection, the influence of parts of the Affordable Rent Act, and box 3 rules are reasons for sale.

### 5.3 The Fraternity Gatekeeper and the Hospiteren Filter

**This is the critical, underreported mechanism that distorts the statistics.** The *hospiteren* system is not a neutral allocation mechanism. It is a social filter that determines who has access to the affordable segment of the student housing market. DUWO, with over 33,000 rooms, has acknowledged that non-members of student associations have "far fewer chances" of obtaining a room. The SSH director has stated that the system "structurally excludes certain groups of students" and creates "increasing inequality".

The data confirms this. In Leiden, **40% of *hospiteer* rooms** are exclusively occupied by members of four fraternities that represent only 16% of the student population. The *hospiteren* system functions as a closed network: fraternity houses select their own members, and rejected non-members are left to the open market. One fraternity member described the process: "Usually you have a good chance that you'll get it... When a house doesn't see a match, the process doesn't stop there. Then they say: 'we don't see it happening', but they do think along with you... Then you get forwarded to other houses".

**The statistical consequence is profound.** The "average" rent figures reported by Kamernet and Nibud are derived from a market that includes a substantial volume of rooms allocated through fraternity networks at below-market or average prices. These rooms are not available to non-members. Therefore, the average is **structurally depressed** by a segment of the market that is inaccessible to the very students who are most in need of affordable housing.

A student who does not join a fraternity—whether by choice, exclusion, or inability to participate in *ontgroening* and the associated social rituals—is not a candidate for the average rent. They are forced into the **residual market**: private landlords, commercial platforms, and the highest-priced segment. They are not average cases; they are **forced high-price cases**.

The student who refuses to pay the compliance price—hazing, sexual ranking and coercion, participation in informal economies—is not making a lifestyle choice. They are accepting a **structural penalty**: exclusion from the affordable segment and forced entry into the expensive one. The statistics do not capture this penalty. They report an average that the non-participant never sees.

### 5.4 The International Student as Statistical Prosthetic

Almost every international student is registered as *uitwonend* (living independently). Of the approximately 130,000 international students in the Netherlands, **94% are *uitwonend***. They must be. They have no parental home in the Netherlands to register at. The DUO system requires a registered address other than the parents' address to qualify for the higher grant; for international students, every address is other than their parents' address.

International students also face systematic exclusion from the fraternity network. One in three international students reports experiencing discrimination in housing, with advertisements explicitly stating "no internationals" or "Dutch only". More than half believe they pay higher rents than Dutch students, and research confirms they pay between **€70 and €85 more per month** for the same room.

This has a profound effect on the statistics. When CBS or Kences reports that "44% of students live independently," that figure includes international students who are *uitwonend* by definition and who pay a premium for their exclusion from the network. Remove them from the calculation, and the proportion of Dutch students living independently falls dramatically.

### 5.5 The Wealthy Dutch Independent: Parental Property as a Hidden Subsidy

The Dutch students who remain in the independent category are not a random sample. They are disproportionately those whose parents have the capital to purchase a home for them. Nibud has documented the growing dependence of students on parental support. In 2024, **57% of students received money from their parents**, with an average monthly contribution of **€225**. Students without a supplementary grant or with a low grant receive a higher direct parental contribution than students with a supplementary grant.

Crucially, the Tweede Kamer has noted that uitwonende HBO and WO students who do not borrow and receive no parental contribution or supplementary grant come up an average of **€532 per month short** to cover the minimum costs of study and living. The loan system did not create independence. It created a system in which parental wealth became the decisive variable in whether a student could live independently at all.

### 5.6 The Hidden Shortage: Students Who Have Given Up

The most damning statistical distortion is the "hidden shortage." Thousands of students have stopped searching for housing altogether because they consider the market hopeless. Kences director Reijnder Jan Spits has confirmed this mechanism: when inventory hits zero, students drop out of sight. Conversely, if new rooms are constructed, latent demand instantly spikes as inactive searchers re-enter the market. Kences states: "We see in new construction projects that students who had given up hope are actively searching again".

The National Student Union (LSVb) has estimated that there are currently around **23,000 student rooms too few**, and that 22,000 students could not find a room last autumn. The shortage could reach **63,000 by 2032**. A 2025 Kences monitor found that **43% of students living at home have stopped searching for rooms altogether because it is too expensive**. The hidden shortage is the statistical residue of a system that has failed so comprehensively that its victims no longer register as victims.

### 5.7 The Composite Effect: What the Statistics Actually Show

When these distortions are combined, the picture is clear. The statistic "44% of students live independently" does not mean that 44% of Dutch students have achieved independence through a functioning system. It means that:

- A large and growing proportion of the independent category consists of international students who are *uitwonend* by necessity and who pay a premium for their exclusion from the network.
- A significant proportion of the remaining Dutch independent students are supported by parental wealth, often through property ownership that converts housing into an income stream.
- A substantial portion of the affordable housing supply is allocated through fraternity networks that are closed to non-members and gated by social compliance, depressing the reported "average" rent and concealing the true market price faced by outsiders.
- A smaller and shrinking proportion are genuinely independent Dutch students without parental support, and these students face structural deficits that force them into debt, precarious work, or both—while paying the non-network price.
- An unknown number of Dutch students have given up searching altogether and are invisible in the statistics.

The system does not produce independent students. It produces a statistical category called "independent students" that is filled by different populations depending on who can afford to enter it or who is granted access by the fraternity gatekeeper. The Dutch student without parental support and without fraternity membership is being priced out of the category—and the category is being filled by those whose independence is subsidised by wealth, by the absence of any alternative, or by network access purchased with compliance.

**The statistics are not a measurement error. They are an instrument of the exclusion.** They produce an image of a functioning system while concealing the mechanism that excludes those without family capital or social compliance.

## 6. The Childcare Benefits Scandal: Institutional Racism as Policy

The most documented evidence of enforced moral hierarchy is the *toeslagenschandaal*—the childcare benefits scandal. On 25 May 2022, the Dutch government publicly admitted for the first time that **institutional racism** in part of the Dutch Tax and Customs Administration was the root cause of the scandal. This admission followed the resignation of the Dutch government in 2021.

The mechanism was algorithmic. To create risk profiles of individuals applying for childcare benefits, the Tax and Customs Administration used algorithms in which **"foreign sounding names"** and **"dual nationality"** were used as indicators of potential fraud. The result was that thousands of racialised low- and middle-income families were subjected to scrutiny, falsely accused of fraud, and asked to repay benefits they had obtained completely legally.

Since 2005, thousands of innocent families suffered financial hardship due to the structure of the childcare allowance system. Regulations required that minor or even incomprehensible administrative mistakes could compel families to repay their annual received benefits in full, frequently amounting to tens of thousands of euros; amounts out of reach for many of them. The Tax and Customs Administration (Belastingdienst), incentivised to maximise revenue, conducted an expansive and proactive investigation into suspected fraud. This process led to erroneous benefit terminations, illegal blacklisting, systematic disregard of parental appeals and the subsequent out-of-home placement of **2,090 children**.

Subsequent exposés uncovered that the Dutch Tax and Customs Administration employed discriminatory profiling practices to hunt for new cases of suspected fraud, disproportionately targeting individuals with non-Dutch and/or immigrant backgrounds. These actions prompted allegations of systemic discrimination within the tax authority. Despite issued warnings in 2012 and 2014 regarding the severity of the Tax and Customs Administration's recovery policy, responsible officials failed to act and members of the cabinet did not inform Parliament about these urgent concerns.

Two ensuing parliamentary inquiries concluded that human rights were overlooked across all arms of government in an increasingly uncompromising political and social context. The reports determined that fundamental rights had been violated and the rule of law undermined. They further highlighted that the social security and allowance systems intended to aid citizens instead caused substantial harm and that the tax authority acted prejudicial. Concurrently, the Parliamentary Inquiry Committee on Fraud Policy and Service Provision identified **"institutional racism"** as a contributing factor.

This was not an error. It was the operation of a system that used racial and national markers as proxies for fraud. The state apparatus was deployed to discipline a specific population—those with migration backgrounds—while the moral narrative of Dutch honesty and law-abidingness remained intact. And for the students in these families, the effect was to destroy any possibility of upwards mobility. The parents' debt became the child's inheritance. The home was lost. The capital that might have supported a student through university was confiscated.

The mechanism of exclusion operates across generations. Excluding the parent excludes the child.

## 7. The DUO Algorithm: Disciplining Students by Background

The enforcement mechanism extends to education. The Dutch Education Executive Agency (DUO) used an algorithm between 2012 and 2023 to check whether students were abusing the grant for students living away from home. The Dutch Data Protection Authority concluded that this approach was **discriminatory and therefore unlawful**.

The algorithm assigned a "risk score" based on type of education, distance between addresses, and age. There was no objective justification for these criteria. Furthermore, the Minister of Education, Culture and Science acknowledged that the algorithm had **indirectly discriminated** students with a migration background. A follow-up investigation commissioned by the Ministry of Education revealed that **86% of students who objected to DUO's decisions had a non-European migration background**.

DUO calculated with an algorithm the risk that a student would commit fraud with the uitwonendenbeurs. DUO selected students for a home visit using these criteria: Education type: an MBO programme gave a higher risk score than an HBO or WO programme. Distance: a shorter distance between the student's residential address and that of the parent(s) gave a higher risk score. Age: a lower age of the student gave a higher risk score. With a higher risk score, a student could (after manual selection by DUO) more often face a check and a home visit from DUO.

The Dutch DPA estimated that between 2013 and 2022, DUO selected **21,500 students** and checked them for fraud, partly on the basis of the algorithm's calculations. Approximately **10,000 students** are now receiving compensation because the fraud investigations were based on discriminatory selection. The government has set aside **€61 million** to compensate all current and former students who were fined by DUO and had to pay back their basic student grant.

This is the mechanism in operation. The state identifies a population—students with migration backgrounds—and deploys its enforcement apparatus disproportionately against them. Meanwhile, the student in Scenario C, whose parents own property and generate rental income, is not subject to the same scrutiny. The algorithm does not flag wealth; it flags foreignness. Upwards mobility becomes not just difficult but dangerous—the higher you climb, the more likely you are to be investigated.

## 8. The Welfare Fraud Act: Criminalising the Powerless

While the state deploys algorithms to target racialised populations, it simultaneously enforces a punitive regime against welfare recipients. Hertogh (2024) argues that the Dutch Welfare Fraud Act of 2012 significantly intensified sanctions for "fraudulent" behaviour of welfare recipients. This Act introduced fines of up to **€50,000**, and made it possible for fraudsters to be entirely excluded from certain welfare services. Behaviours such as failing to disclose relevant information or refusing certain job offers were explicitly classified as prohibited.

Crucially, eligibility for benefits was not only conditional on compliance with these rules, but violations could trigger new punishments. Convicts of welfare fraud were not just punished by the obligation to reimburse earlier payments, but could also receive additional fines or even prison sentences. Beyond these formal penalties, the stigma of being labelled a "fraudster" often resulted in long-term exclusion from insurance, bank accounts, housing, and employment.

This culminated in the 2014 Fraud Act, which introduced a system of high sanction in all cases of benefit fraud, even if they were caused by administrative errors. Public debate was further fuelled by the life-changing severity of the punishments inflicted on the victims. Pieter Omtzigt, one of the members of parliament who brought the scandal to light, emphasised that beyond ethnic profiling and intensified welfare fraud investigations, two other practices also contributed to the scandal. First, there was an attitudinal issue amongst the tax inspectors such that even the smallest mistakes were interpreted as evidence of fraud and penalised with the maximum fines permitted by law. Ten Hooven (2021) reported that **something as minor as a missing signature could result in the confiscation of housing and financial assets**.

This relates to the second problem: the rigid reimbursement policy. Accused recipients were required to repay all childcare benefits in full and immediately without the usual legal proceedings.

This is the double standard in operation. The state uses algorithms that disproportionately target migration backgrounds for fraud investigation, while simultaneously imposing draconian penalties on those who receive welfare—the poorest and most vulnerable. The message is that fraud by the powerful is a business model; fraud by the powerless is a crime. The student in Scenario B, who faces a structural deficit, is a potential target. The student in Scenario C, whose parents' wealth generates income, is not.

## 9. Moral Superiority as Enforcement: The 7 Vinkjes and Calvinist Chosenness

The enforcement mechanism is not only legal and administrative. It is also cultural and moral. The Dutch self-image—constructed over centuries—positions the Netherlands as moderate, rational, and civilised. This self-image is rooted in Calvinism, which shaped the Dutch Republic and provided a framework in which Protestants saw themselves as the **chosen people**.

The contemporary expression of this moral hierarchy is the **"7 vinkjes"**—the seven checkmarks identified by journalist Joris Luyendijk. In his book, *De zeven Vinkjes – Hoe mannen zoals ik de baas spelen* ("The Seven Check Marks – How men like me play boss"), Luyendijk argues that it is high time that privileged people like him make room for people with fewer privileges. By "people like me", he means male (check), white (check), straight (check), who has at least one parent born in the Netherlands, at least one highly educated parent earning good money, a certain social status or cultural capital, and a university degree (check, check, check, check). People with seven check marks should make room for the ones who are not always given the benefit of the doubt because they are seen as the Other.

Luyendijk's list of check marks is incomplete. His book fails to mention several privileges that significantly affect a person's opportunities and position. For instance: Being cisgender, being able-bodied and without chronic illnesses, having no discernable religion. People with seven check marks who are transgender, use a wheelchair, can't work for more than five hours a day or always wear a yarmulke will still have fewer opportunities to "play boss".

The 7 vinkjes are not merely a description of privilege. They are the operating system of the moral hierarchy. Those who possess all seven can afford to be honest because their position is secured by social capital. Those who lack them are systematically disadvantaged and, when they attempt to navigate the system, are met with suspicion. The student in Scenario C likely possesses most or all of the 7 vinkjes. The student in Scenario B likely does not.

The moral hierarchy converts structural exclusion into personal failure. The excluded student is not told that the system was designed to exclude them; they are told that they did not work hard enough, did not plan well enough, did not choose the right programme. The moral narrative is the final instrument of exclusion: it makes the excluded complicit in their own exclusion by convincing them it was their choice.

The same logic applies to the fraternity gatekeeper. The student who is excluded from the network rent is not told that the network is gated by compliance. They are told that they simply did not find a room. The compliance price—the hazing, the ranking, the informal economies—is not presented as a price. It is presented as "student life," as "tradition," as "belonging." The student who declines to pay it is not told that they have declined to pay it. They are told that they failed to secure housing.

## 10. Kruiwagen Culture: The Labour Market as a Closed Network

The moral hierarchy is reinforced by the **kruiwagen** culture—the Dutch term for getting a job or privilege through connections. Dutch labour market experts keep talking about a "matching problem." But the real problem is one rarely named: **Kruiwagen culture**. Jobs arranged through closed circles, quiet referrals, and opportunities handed "onder het tafel" (under the table). Call it what it is: **NEPOTISM dressed up as normal hiring**.

The *kruiwagen* is a wheelbarrow, but in its pejorative sense it refers to someone who helps another obtain a position or privilege. In the Dutch labour market, this means that access to jobs—especially desirable ones—is mediated through networks that are closed to outsiders. Those inside the network can afford to be honest because their position is secured. Those outside must find other means to survive.

For many—women of colour, African professionals, internationals—the barrier isn't skill; it's access. You're filtered out before a human even looks at your CV. HR systems reward familiarity, not capability. Companies shout about staff shortages while ignoring qualified candidates who don't fit the "inner circle" of "7 vinkjes" who might be upstaged by internationals professionals who might have better qualifications. Insiders move job to job, while others are told there's a "skills mismatch." And layered on top is the "7 vinkjes" effect—the well-documented Dutch privilege template. **If you match all seven, doors open. If you don't, the system quietly closes ranks**.

The combination of the *kruiwagen* and the 7 vinkjes produces a system where the established class can afford to be honest because the system is designed to protect them, while the working class is left to compete for scraps. The student in Scenario C inherits the *kruiwagen*. The student in Scenario B must build it from nothing, and when they fail, they are blamed for the failure.

The exclusion is complete: the student without family capital is excluded from housing (by rent and the fraternity gatekeeper), from stability (by deficit), from honest opportunity (by closed networks). Each instrument reinforces the others.

## 11. Discussion: The Mechanism Is Not Accidental

The evidence presented in this paper supports the proposition that the mechanism is not accidental—it is enforced. The enforcement operates through:

1. **Grant structure** that assumes a parental contribution the student does not have, producing a structural deficit of €723–€941 per month.
2. **Housing market regulation** that requires guarantors and deposits, excluding students without family capital before they even reach the rent.
3. **The fraternity and *hospiteren* gatekeeper** that segments the housing market, granting affordable access to network members who pay the compliance price—hazing, sexual ranking and coercion, participation in informal economies—while forcing non-members into the highest-price segment, and thereby depressing the reported "average" rent that conceals this penalty.
4. **Algorithmic profiling** that disproportionately targets racialised populations for fraud investigation.
5. **Punitive welfare legislation** that criminalises administrative error and imposes devastating sanctions on the poorest and most vulnerable.
6. **Moral hierarchy** rooted in Calvinist notions of chosenness that legitimises the exclusion of outsiders and positions the Dutch elite as morally superior.
7. **Kruiwagen culture** that allocates jobs through closed networks, ensuring that the established class maintains its position while outsiders are left to compete for scraps.

These instruments are not separate. They are interconnected. The grant structure creates the deficit. The housing market and fraternity gatekeeper convert the deficit into exclusion while concealing it in the statistics. The algorithmic profiling identifies who is suspect. The welfare legislation determines who is punished. The moral hierarchy justifies the differential treatment. The *kruiwagen* ensures that those who are not suspect are rewarded with jobs and security.

**It is the system functioning as designed to exclude those without family capital or network access.**

The self-referential dimension of this analysis is that it emerged from a first-person account of navigating this system. The account described a student who received €1,000 from DUO, paid €900 in rent—a non-network price—and was left with €100 for food and all other expenses. But the €100 was not the full measure of the deficit. Measured against the Nibud minimum budget of €1,723 per month (excluding tuition), the student faced a **monthly deficit of €723**. The student bridged this gap through loans and work—because the formal system provided no path. When the student refused to participate in the only opportunities available, they were left without a degree and without a path.

The evidence confirms that this is not an isolated experience. It is the predictable outcome of a system that enforces differential treatment through legal, administrative, cultural, and statistical mechanisms. The three scenarios demonstrate the structural asymmetry. The student in Scenario B is not failing because of personal deficiency. They are failing because the system is designed for Scenario C—and because the statistics that claim to describe their situation are derived from a market they cannot access.

**There is no upwards mobility for the student without parental support (a home) and without fraternity network access. It is the system functioning as designed to exclude those without family capital or social compliance.**

The system does not provide a ladder. It provides a sieve. Those with family capital or network access pass through; those without are filtered out. The enforcement apparatus ensures that the filter is not just economic but also legal, administrative, moral, and statistical. The student without parental support is not just poor; they are suspect. They are not just struggling; they are excluded by design.

The statistics that purport to measure the system's functioning are themselves instruments of this exclusion. By including international students, wealthy independents, and fraternity members in the "independent student" category, they produce an image of a system that works for a majority, while concealing the structural devastation experienced by the minority who are genuinely independent, genuinely without support, and genuinely outside the network.

## 12. Conclusion

The Dutch state operates a system of enforcement that is not neutral. It targets certain populations for scrutiny while protecting others. It criminalises the administrative errors of the poor while the structural fraud of institutions—empty classrooms funded by tax money, discriminatory algorithms defended as risk management—remains largely uninvestigated. It maintains a moral hierarchy that positions the established class as honest and law-abiding while casting outsiders as suspect. And it maintains a housing market segmented by fraternity networks that grant affordable access to insiders who pay the compliance price—hazing, sexual ranking and coercion, participation in informal economies—while forcing outsiders into the highest-price segment, all while reporting an "average" rent that no non-member ever sees.

The evidence is not speculative. It is documented in parliamentary inquiries, court rulings, scholarly analysis, and the admissions of housing providers themselves. The childcare benefits scandal, the DUO algorithm, the Welfare Fraud Act, the 7 vinkjes, the *hospiteren* system, and kruiwagen culture are not isolated phenomena. They are instruments of a single mechanism: the enforcement of a class and racial hierarchy that benefits the established Dutch elite while excluding those who fall outside the 7 vinkjes or the fraternity network.

The three student scenarios illustrate the mechanism in concrete terms. The student living with lower-class parents has a positive budget and low risk, but limited mobility. The student living independently on DUO with €900 rent faces a monthly deficit of €723 against the Nibud minimum budget, and no mobility at all—and their €900 rent is not the average but the non-network price. The student living in a parent-purchased home with rental income has abundant disposable income, no risk, and high mobility—and extracts that income from the rents paid by the students in the second scenario.

**There is no upwards mobility for the student without parental support (a home) and without fraternity network access. It is the system functioning as designed to exclude those without family capital or social compliance.**

This is the central finding. The system is not designed to enable transcendence of class position; it is designed to reproduce it. The student who lacks a home and lacks network access is trapped in a cycle of survival, unable to save, unable to invest, unable to build the capital that would allow them to escape. The student who has a home is on an escalator, accumulating assets and connections that compound over time. The gap between them is not a gap in effort or intelligence; it is a gap in structure. And that structure is enforced—economically, legally, culturally, and statistically.

The mechanism is not accidental. It is enforced. And those who refuse to pay the compliance price—who refuse to be hazed, who refuse to participate in or tolerate the ranking and coercion of women, who refuse to participate in the informal economies of the fraternity house—are left without a degree, without a home, and without a path. That is not a personal failure. It is the system functioning as designed. The system does not want upwards mobility for those without parental support or network access. It wants compliance. And when compliance is not forthcoming, it wants exclusion.

**It is the system functioning as designed to exclude those without family capital or social compliance.**
